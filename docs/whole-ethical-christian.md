### What “ethical” means

In this project, *ethical* is a technical term: ethics consists of the rules of interaction between one or more ethoses. An ethical reading of the New Testament therefore asks what kinds of relations its texts establish between persons, communities, representatives, and institutions acting from or on behalf of an ethos—what they may require, offer, refuse, judge, exchange, or repair. Virtues are used only as a secondary instrument when an interaction depends on how an ethos defines or ranks them.

### What counts as an ethos

An ethos is a Logos belonging to a person or group whose principal content concerns what ought to be. It is not an isolated belief, commandment, virtue, or action, but an identifiable governing whole containing declarations, priorities, conditions of belonging and representation, and enough continuity to guide conduct across different situations. A complete ethos must be publicly knowable, internally coherent, and provide ways for others to understand, adopt, evaluate, or challenge it. A single New Testament passage may reveal part of an ethos, but it does not constitute the whole ethos by itself.

### What this project is reconstructing

This project reconstructs a sequence of related but non-identical ethoses. It begins with the ethos taught and enacted by Jesus during his life, while keeping each Gospel witness distinguishable. It then examines the ethos shared in the name of Christ after Jesus' death, including apostolic enactment and the Didachean codification, before isolating the Pauline upgrade, post-Pauline upgrades, and present popular Christian ethoses. Each later layer may preserve, specify, extend, reorder, or contradict an earlier one; it is never treated as Jesus' own ethos merely because it later became Christian. Revelation is outside the present scope.

### Method of extraction

Each study begins with a defined textual unit and its parallels, identifies the speaker, addressees, represented groups, and relevant institutions, and then extracts the declaration, interaction rule, membership condition, role, judgment procedure, or governance protocol expressed by the text. The proposed element is mapped to the Theory of Ethos—its principles, phylogeny, compatibility conditions, hierarchy of intimacy, and five ethical roles—and compared with its enactment and consequences elsewhere in the New Testament. Virtue analysis is introduced only where the passage orders competing ends or where such an ordering is needed to explain the extracted rule.

### Status of claims and challenges

Every reconstructed element is presented as a claim supported by specified textual evidence and assigned a status: **explicit**, **strongly implied**, **tentative**, **contested**, or **contradicted elsewhere**. Alternative readings and counter-evidence remain attached to the claim rather than being concealed through harmonization. Challenges must identify the claim being disputed, provide evidence or expose a contradiction, and propose a precise correction when possible. Accepted challenges change the reconstruction, while unresolved ones remain public as open questions.

*The Lord's teaching to the heathen by the Twelve Apostles.*

> **Textual source:** *The Didache*, translated by Kirsopp Lake in *The Apostolic Fathers*, vol. 1 (1912). This is a public-domain English translation. The Markdown text is derived from the [Wikisource transcription](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Didache_%28Lake_translation%29). Editorial changes are limited to chapter headings, verse labels and anchors, and paragraph formatting. Lake's original notes are retained below; his marginal section summaries are omitted.

## Chapter 1

<a id="didache-1-1"></a>**1:1** There are two Ways, one of Life and one of Death, and there is a great difference between the two Ways.

<a id="didache-1-2"></a>**1:2** The Way of Life is this: "First, thou shalt love the God who made thee, secondly, thy neighbour as thyself; and whatsoever thou wouldst not have done to thyself, do not thou to another."[^1]

<a id="didache-1-3"></a>**1:3** Now, the teaching of these words is this: "Bless those that curse you, and pray for your enemies, and fast for those that persecute you. For what credit is it to you if you love those that love you? Do not even the heathen do the same?" But, for your part, "love those that hate you," and you will have no enemy.

<a id="didache-1-4"></a>**1:4** "Abstain from carnal" and bodily "lusts." "If any man smite thee on the right cheek, turn to him the other cheek also," and thou wilt be perfect. "If any man impress thee to go with him one mile, go with him two. If any man take thy coat, give him thy shirt also. If any man will take from thee what is thine, refuse it not"—not even if thou canst.[^2]

<a id="didache-1-5"></a>**1:5** Give to everyone that asks thee, and do not refuse, for the Father's will is that we give to all from the gifts we have received. Blessed is he that gives according to the mandate; for he is innocent. Woe to him who receives; for if any man receive alms under pressure of need he is innocent; but he who receives it without need shall be tried as to why he took and for what, and being in prison he shall be examined as to his deeds, and "he shall not come out thence until he pay the last farthing."

<a id="didache-1-6"></a>**1:6** But concerning this it was also said, "Let thine alms sweat into thine hands until thou knowest to whom thou art giving."

## Chapter 2

<a id="didache-2-1"></a>**2:1** But the second commandment of the teaching is this:

<a id="didache-2-2"></a>**2:2** "Thou shalt do no murder; thou shalt not commit adultery"; thou shalt not commit sodomy; thou shalt not commit fornication; thou shalt not steal; thou shalt not use magic; thou shalt not use philtres; thou shalt not procure abortion, nor commit infanticide; "thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's goods";

<a id="didache-2-3"></a>**2:3** thou shalt not commit perjury, "thou shalt not bear false witness"; thou shalt not speak evil; thou shalt not bear malice.

<a id="didache-2-4"></a>**2:4** Thou shalt not be double-minded nor double-tongued, for to be double-tongued is the snare of death.

<a id="didache-2-5"></a>**2:5** Thy speech shall not be false nor vain, but completed in action.

<a id="didache-2-6"></a>**2:6** Thou shalt not be covetous nor extortionate, nor a hypocrite, nor malignant, nor proud; thou shalt make no evil plan against thy neighbour.

<a id="didache-2-7"></a>**2:7** Thou shalt hate no man; but some thou shalt reprove,[^3] and for some shalt thou pray, and some thou shalt love more than thine own life.

## Chapter 3

<a id="didache-3-1"></a>**3:1** My child, flee from every evil man and from all like him.

<a id="didache-3-2"></a>**3:2** Be not proud, for pride leads to murder, nor jealous, nor contentious, nor passionate, for from all these murders are engendered.

<a id="didache-3-3"></a>**3:3** My child, be not lustful, for lust leads to fornication, nor a speaker of base words, nor a lifter up of the eyes, for from all these is adultery engendered.

<a id="didache-3-4"></a>**3:4** My child, regard not omens, for this leads to idolatry; neither be an enchanter, nor an astrologer, nor a magician, neither wish to see these things, for from them all is idolatry engendered.

<a id="didache-3-5"></a>**3:5** My child, be not a liar, for lying leads to theft, nor a lover of money, nor vain-glorious, for from all these things are thefts engendered.

<a id="didache-3-6"></a>**3:6** My child, be not a grumbler, for this leads to blasphemy, nor stubborn, nor a thinker of evil, for from all these are blasphemies engendered,

<a id="didache-3-7"></a>**3:7** but be thou "meek, for the meek shall inherit the earth;"

<a id="didache-3-8"></a>**3:8** be thou long-suffering, and merciful and guileless, and quiet, and good, and ever fearing the words which thou hast heard.

<a id="didache-3-9"></a>**3:9** Thou shalt not exalt thyself, nor let thy soul be presumptuous. Thy soul shall not consort with the lofty, but thou shalt walk with righteous and humble men.

<a id="didache-3-10"></a>**3:10** Receive the accidents that befall to thee as good, knowing that nothing happens without God.

## Chapter 4

<a id="didache-4-1"></a>**4:1** My child, thou shalt remember, day and night, him who speaks the word of God to thee, and thou shalt honour him as the Lord, for where the Lord's nature is spoken of, there is he present.

<a id="didache-4-2"></a>**4:2** And thou shalt seek daily the presence of the saints, that thou mayest find rest in their words.

<a id="didache-4-3"></a>**4:3** Thou shalt not desire a schism, but shalt reconcile those that strive. Thou shalt give righteous judgment; thou shalt favour no man's person in reproving transgression.

<a id="didache-4-4"></a>**4:4** Thou shalt not be of two minds whether it shall be or not.

<a id="didache-4-5"></a>**4:5** Be not one who stretches out his hands to receive, but shuts them when it comes to giving.

<a id="didache-4-6"></a>**4:6** Of whatsoever thou hast gained by thy hands thou shalt give a ransom for thy sins.

<a id="didache-4-7"></a>**4:7** Thou shalt not hesitate to give, nor shalt thou grumble when thou givest, for thou shalt know who is the good Paymaster of the reward.

<a id="didache-4-8"></a>**4:8** Thou shalt not turn away the needy, but shalt share everything with thy brother, and shalt not say that it is thine own, for if you are sharers in the imperishable, how much more in the things which perish?

<a id="didache-4-9"></a>**4:9** Thou shalt not withhold thine hand from thy son or from thy daughter, but thou shalt teach them the fear of God from their youth up.

<a id="didache-4-10"></a>**4:10** Thou shalt not command in thy bitterness thy slave or thine handmaid, who hope in the same God, lest they cease to fear the God who is over you both; for he comes not to call men with respect of persons, but those whom the Spirit has prepared.

<a id="didache-4-11"></a>**4:11** But do you who are slaves be subject to your master, as to God's representative, in reverence and fear.

<a id="didache-4-12"></a>**4:12** Thou shalt hate all hypocrisy, and everything that is not pleasing to the Lord.

<a id="didache-4-13"></a>**4:13** Thou shalt not forsake the commandments of the Lord, but thou shalt keep what thou didst receive, "adding nothing to it and taking nothing away."

<a id="didache-4-14"></a>**4:14** In the congregation thou shalt confess thy transgressions, and thou shalt not betake thyself to prayer with an evil conscience. This is the Way of Life.

## Chapter 5

<a id="didache-5-1"></a>**5:1** But the Way of Death is this: First of all, it is wicked and full of cursing, murders, adulteries, lusts, fornications, thefts, idolatries, witchcrafts, charms, robberies, false witness, hypocrisies, a double heart, fraud, pride, malice, stubbornness, covetousness, foul speech, jealousy, impudence, haughtiness, boastfulness.

<a id="didache-5-2"></a>**5:2** Persecutors of the good, haters of truth, lovers of lies, knowing not the reward of righteousness, not cleaving to the good nor to righteous judgment, spending wakeful nights not for good but for wickedness, from whom meekness and patience is far, lovers of vanity, following after reward, unmerciful to the poor, not working for him who is oppressed with toil, without knowledge of him who made them, murderers of children, corrupters of God's creatures, turning away the needy, oppressing the distressed, advocates of the rich, unjust judges of the poor, altogether sinful; may ye be delivered, my children, from all these.

## Chapter 6

<a id="didache-6-1"></a>**6:1** See "that no one make thee to err" from this Way of the teaching, for he teaches thee without God.

<a id="didache-6-2"></a>**6:2** For if thou canst bear the whole yoke of the Lord, thou wilt be perfect, but if thou canst not, do what thou canst.

<a id="didache-6-3"></a>**6:3** And concerning food, bear what thou canst, but keep strictly from that which is offered to idols, for it is the worship of dead gods.

## Chapter 7

<a id="didache-7-1"></a>**7:1** Concerning baptism, baptise thus: Having first rehearsed all these things, "baptise, in the Name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit," in running water;

<a id="didache-7-2"></a>**7:2** but if thou hast no running water, baptise in other water, and if thou canst not in cold, then in warm.

<a id="didache-7-3"></a>**7:3** But if thou hast neither, pour water three times on the head "in the Name of the Father, Son and Holy Spirit."

<a id="didache-7-4"></a>**7:4** And before the baptism let the baptiser and him who is to be baptised fast, and any others who are able. And thou shalt bid him who is to be baptised to fast one or two days before.

## Chapter 8

<a id="didache-8-1"></a>**8:1** Let not your fasts be with the hypocrites, for they fast on Mondays and Thursdays, but do you fast on Wednesdays and Fridays.

<a id="didache-8-2"></a>**8:2** And do not pray as the hypocrites, but as the Lord commanded in his Gospel, pray thus: "Our Father, who art in Heaven, hallowed be thy Name, thy Kingdom come, thy will be done, as in Heaven so also upon earth; give us to-day our daily[^4] bread, and forgive us our debt as we forgive our debtors, and lead us not into trial, but deliver us from the Evil One, for thine is the power and the glory for ever."

<a id="didache-8-3"></a>**8:3** Pray thus three times a day.

## Chapter 9

<a id="didache-9-1"></a>**9:1** And concerning the Eucharist, hold[^5] Eucharist thus:

<a id="didache-9-2"></a>**9:2** First concerning the Cup, "We give thanks to thee, our Father, for the Holy Vine of David thy child, which, thou didst make known to us through Jesus thy child; to thee be glory for ever."

<a id="didache-9-3"></a>**9:3** And concerning the broken Bread: "We give thee thanks, our Father, for the life and knowledge which thou didst make known to us through Jesus thy Child. To thee be glory for ever.

<a id="didache-9-4"></a>**9:4** As this broken bread was scattered upon the mountains, but was brought together and became one, so let thy Church be gathered together from the ends of the earth into thy Kingdom, for thine is the glory and the power through Jesus Christ for ever."

<a id="didache-9-5"></a>**9:5** But let none eat or drink of your Eucharist except those who have been baptised in the Lord's Name. For concerning this also did the Lord say, "Give not that which is holy to the dogs."

## Chapter 10

<a id="didache-10-1"></a>**10:1** But after you are satisfied with food, thus give thanks:

<a id="didache-10-2"></a>**10:2** "We give thanks to thee, O Holy Father, for thy Holy Name which thou didst make to tabernacle in our hearts, and for the knowledge and faith and immortality which thou didst make known to us through Jesus thy Child. To thee be glory for ever.

<a id="didache-10-3"></a>**10:3** Thou, Lord Almighty, didst create all things for thy Name's sake, and didst give food and drink to men for their enjoyment, that they might give thanks to thee, but us hast thou blessed with spiritual food and drink and eternal light through thy Child.

<a id="didache-10-4"></a>**10:4** Above all we give thanks to thee for that thou art mighty. To thee be glory for ever.

<a id="didache-10-5"></a>**10:5** Remember, Lord, thy Church, to deliver it from all evil and to make it perfect in thy love, and gather it together in its holiness from the four winds to thy kingdom which thou hast prepared for it. For thine is the power and the glory for ever.

<a id="didache-10-6"></a>**10:6** Let grace come and let this world pass away. Hosannah to the God of David. If any man be holy, let him come! if any man be not, let him repent: Maran atha,[^6] Amen."

<a id="didache-10-7"></a>**10:7** But suffer the prophets to hold Eucharist as they will.

## Chapter 11

<a id="didache-11-1"></a>**11:1** Whosoever then comes and teaches you all these things aforesaid, receive him.

<a id="didache-11-2"></a>**11:2** But if the teacher himself be perverted and teach another doctrine to destroy these things, do not listen to him, but if his teaching be for the increase of righteousness and knowledge of the Lord, receive him as the Lord.

<a id="didache-11-3"></a>**11:3** And concerning the Apostles and Prophets, act thus according to the ordinance of the Gospel.[^7]

<a id="didache-11-4"></a>**11:4** Let every Apostle who comes to you be received as the Lord,

<a id="didache-11-5"></a>**11:5** but let him not stay more than one day, or if need be a second as well; but if he stay three days, he is a false prophet.

<a id="didache-11-6"></a>**11:6** And when an Apostle goes forth let him accept nothing but bread till he reach his night's lodging; but if he ask for money, he is a false prophet.

<a id="didache-11-7"></a>**11:7** Do not test or examine any prophet who is speaking in a spirit, "for every sin shall be forgiven, but this sin shall not be forgiven."

<a id="didache-11-8"></a>**11:8** But not everyone who speaks in a spirit is a prophet, except he have the behaviour of the Lord. From his behaviour, then, the false prophet and the true prophet shall be known.

<a id="didache-11-9"></a>**11:9** And no prophet who orders a meal in a spirit shall eat of it: otherwise he is a false prophet.

<a id="didache-11-10"></a>**11:10** And every prophet who teaches the truth, if he do not what he teaches, is a false prophet.

<a id="didache-11-11"></a>**11:11** But no prophet who has been tried and is genuine, though he enact a worldly mystery[^8] of the Church, if he teach not others to do what he does himself, shall be judged by you: for he has his judgment with God, for so also did the prophets of old.

<a id="didache-11-12"></a>**11:12** But whosoever shall say in a spirit 'Give me money, or something else,' you shall not listen to him; but if he tell you to give on behalf of others in want, let none judge him.

## Chapter 12

<a id="didache-12-1"></a>**12:1** Let everyone who "comes in the Name of the Lord" be received; but when you have tested him you shall know him, for you shall have understanding of true and false.[^9]

<a id="didache-12-2"></a>**12:2** If he who comes is a traveller, help him as much as you can, but he shall not remain with you more than two days, or, if need be, three.

<a id="didache-12-3"></a>**12:3** And if he wishes to settle among you and has a craft, let him work for his bread.

<a id="didache-12-4"></a>**12:4** But if he has no craft provide for him according to your understanding, so that no man shall live among you in idleness because he is a Christian.

<a id="didache-12-5"></a>**12:5** But if he will not do so, he is making traffic of Christ; beware of such.

## Chapter 13

<a id="didache-13-1"></a>**13:1** But every true prophet who wishes to settle among you is "worthy of his food."

<a id="didache-13-2"></a>**13:2** Likewise a true teacher is himself worthy, like the workman, of his food.

<a id="didache-13-3"></a>**13:3** Therefore thou shalt take the firstfruit of the produce of the winepress and of the threshing-floor and of oxen and sheep, and shalt give them as the firstfruits to the prophets, for they are your high priests.

<a id="didache-13-4"></a>**13:4** But if you have not a prophet, give to the poor.

<a id="didache-13-5"></a>**13:5** If thou makest bread, take the firstfruits, and give it according to the commandment.

<a id="didache-13-6"></a>**13:6** Likewise when thou openest a jar of wine or oil, give the firstfruits to the prophets.

<a id="didache-13-7"></a>**13:7** Of money also and clothes, and of all your possessions, take the firstfruits, as it seem best to you, and give according to the commandment.

## Chapter 14

<a id="didache-14-1"></a>**14:1** On the Lord's Day of the Lord come together, break bread and hold Eucharist, after confessing your transgressions that your offering may be pure;

<a id="didache-14-2"></a>**14:2** but let none who has a quarrel with his fellow join in your meeting until they be reconciled, that your sacrifice be not defiled.

<a id="didache-14-3"></a>**14:3** For this is that which was spoken by the Lord, "In every place and time offer me a pure sacrifice, for I am a great king," saith the Lord, "and my name is wonderful among the heathen."

## Chapter 15

<a id="didache-15-1"></a>**15:1** Appoint therefore for yourselves bishops and deacons worthy of the Lord, meek men, and not lovers of money, and truthful and approved, for they also minister to you the ministry of the prophets and teachers.

<a id="didache-15-2"></a>**15:2** Therefore do not despise them, for they are your honourable men together with the prophets and teachers.

<a id="didache-15-3"></a>**15:3** And reprove one another not in wrath but in peace as you find in the Gospel, and let none speak with any who has done a wrong to his neighbour, nor let him hear a word from you until he repents.

<a id="didache-15-4"></a>**15:4** But your prayers and alms and all your acts perform as ye find in the Gospel of our Lord.

## Chapter 16

<a id="didache-16-1"></a>**16:1** "Watch" over your life: "let your lamps" be not quenched "and your loins" be not ungirded, but be "ready," for ye know not "the hour in which our Lord cometh."

<a id="didache-16-2"></a>**16:2** But be frequently gathered together seeking the things which are profitable for your souls, for the whole time of your faith shall not profit you except ye be found perfect at the last time;

<a id="didache-16-3"></a>**16:3** for in the last days the false prophets and the corrupters shall be multiplied, and the sheep shall be turned into wolves, and love shall change to hate;

<a id="didache-16-4"></a>**16:4** for as lawlessness increaseth they shall hate one another and persecute and betray, and then shall appear the deceiver of the world as a Son of God, and shall do signs and wonders and the earth shall be given over into his hands and he shall commit iniquities which have never been since the world began.

<a id="didache-16-5"></a>**16:5** Then shall the creation of mankind come to the fiery trial and "many shall be offended" and be lost, but "they who endure" in their faith "shall be saved" by the curse itself.[^10]

<a id="didache-16-6"></a>**16:6** And "then shall appear the signs" of the truth. First the sign spread out in Heaven, then the sign of the sound of the trumpet, and thirdly the resurrection of the dead:

<a id="didache-16-7"></a>**16:7** but not of all the dead, but as it was said, "The Lord shall come and all his saints with him."

<a id="didache-16-8"></a>**16:8** Then shall the world "see the Lord coming on the clouds of Heaven."

# The Ethos of Jesus

This section reconstructs the governing whole taught and enacted by Jesus during his life: its public declarations, interaction rules, membership, allocation of responsibility and resources, treatment of other ethoses, standards of proof, and forms of authority. It uses all four Gospels as witnesses without pretending that they are one report. A claim enters the reconstruction only with its witnesses and status attached.

“The ethos of Jesus” is narrower than “the ethos of Christ.” The former concerns what the Gospel narratives attribute to Jesus' earthly teaching and action. Instructions placed after the resurrection may testify to intended continuity, but their communal enactment belongs to the post-death ethos of Christ. This boundary prevents later apostolic and Pauline structures from being read backward as if Jesus had already stated them.

The present reconstruction is provisional. Its strongest initial findings concern allegiance, enacted proof, positive reciprocity, responsibility for representatives, challenge procedures, missionary dependence, servant authority, treatment of opponents, and the conditions for joining or leaving.

---

# Reconstructed Ethos

This is the synthesis layer. It assembles only claims established in the detailed studies and keeps their evidential status visible. It does not turn every instruction into an ethos declaration: a declaration must contribute to the governing whole by specifying allegiance, interaction, membership, representation, judgment, resources, responsibility, or change.

The reconstruction presently indicates a public and enacted ethos under one ultimate master; positive initiative toward others; costly but voluntary discipleship; delegated mission; non-dominating authority; behavioral tests for representatives; correction with escalation and witnesses; and active treatment of neighbors, outsiders, and enemies. Whether these parts constitute a complete, internally consistent ethos remains an audit question.

---

# Declarations

The current reconstruction contains the following high-level declarations. These are compact names for conclusions argued elsewhere, not replacements for their textual witnesses.

| Declaration | Current formulation | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `one_master` | Ultimate allegiance cannot be divided between incompatible ruling ethoses. | explicit |
| `constitutive_priority` | Love of God and neighbor governs subordinate requirements. | explicit |
| `positive_reciprocity` | Initiate toward others the treatment one would properly seek from them. | explicit core; qualification contested |
| `proof_by_fruits` | Representatives are evaluated by enacted results, not profession alone. | explicit |
| `enemy_positive_action` | Opposition does not cancel the duty to act positively toward the opponent. | explicit |
| `servant_governance` | Authority among disciples is proved by service rather than domination. | explicit |
| `authorized_representation` | Receiving or rejecting an authorized messenger reaches the sender represented. | explicit |
| `graduated_correction` | An alleged wrong is addressed privately, then with witnesses, then by the assembly. | explicit in Matthew |

The table is not yet a complete constitution. Jesus does not leave one document that names all offices, proof standards, membership conditions, amendment rules, or remedies. Those elements must be reconstructed with their scope limitations visible.

---

# Interaction Rules

Jesus' ethos is most visible where one actor's conduct changes another actor's ethical position. Its main reconstructed interactions presently include:

- member → neighbor or enemy: initiate beneficial treatment without waiting for reciprocity;
- sender → representative: authorize a limited mission and remain implicated in its reception;
- representative → household: offer a message and peace, accept hospitality, and withdraw after refusal rather than compel entry;
- member → member who wrongs: seek repair privately before widening judgment;
- assembly → disputed member: hear witnesses, decide compatibility, and change the relationship if correction fails;
- leader → members: model service and carry the burdens created by authority;
- audience → claimant: test profession by conduct and results.

These rules vary in addressee and institutional scale. A command to an individual disciple cannot automatically be imposed on a state, and a protocol given to a gathered community cannot automatically be reduced to private feeling. The detailed pages identify the level at which each rule operates.

---

# Membership Rules

Membership in Jesus' circle is joinable but costly. Calls to follow are invitations that reorganize work, property, family priority, safety, and allegiance; sayings about calculating a building or a campaign require prospective members to see the cost before commitment. The narratives also show refusals and departures without a procedure for physical compulsion.

The evidence does not support one simple initiation rule for Jesus' lifetime. The Twelve are appointed, other disciples follow, households support the mission, some recipients are healed without joining, and unaffiliated actors can be treated as compatible. Baptism appears in the wider Gospel setting, but the stable baptismal membership of communities acting after Jesus' death belongs to the ethos of Christ.

**Current finding — `strongly implied`:** Jesus' ethos is voluntarily joinable, demands disclosed priority over competing allegiances, and permits factual departure. Whether threats of eschatological loss make it fully “non-binding” under the Theory of Ethos remains contested.

---

# Roles and Authority

Jesus is teacher, sender, judge, healer, and leader within the Gospel narratives. The Twelve and other disciples become learners, messengers, witnesses, and temporary representatives; householders become hosts; crowds remain audiences rather than members merely by listening. A role is established by an assignment and a relation, not by an honorific alone.

Delegated authority is real but bounded. The mission discourses specify message, conduct, dependence, and response to refusal. Reception of a messenger can count as reception of the sender, yet warnings about false prophets and fruit prevent the mere use of Jesus' name from proving authorization. Among disciples, greatness is measured by service, which changes the obligations and proof of rule without eliminating every difference of role.

**Current finding — `strongly implied`:** authority in Jesus' ethos is personal, delegated for defined work, answerable through outcomes, and normatively non-dominating. A complete appointment and removal procedure is not supplied during Jesus' life.

---

# Present Audit

At this stage, the reconstruction has substantial evidence for a minimal identity, missionary responsibility, awareness of mission resources, challenge through questions and correction, partial cooperation with outsiders, acceptance of witnesses, behavioral proof, delegated representation, and servant governance.

Important gaps remain. The Gospels do not present a single public register of declarations; the route for amending the whole ethos is unclear; procedures differ among witnesses; reparations are commanded more clearly than institutional liability is assigned; and post-death baptism, offices, and inter-community government must not be imported into Jesus' lifetime. Apparent tensions concerning outsiders, publicity, family, law, judgment, and departure are still open.

The proper status is therefore **provisional reconstruction**, not “ethically complete ethos.” Completeness can be claimed only after the phylogeny matrix and contradiction audit identify the evidence for every required attribute and answer the strongest counterexamples.

# Minimal and Meta-Ethos

These studies ask whether Jesus' teaching meets the minimum conditions of an identifiable ethos and how it regulates encounters with another ethos. The relevant evidence concerns unified allegiance, public enactment, reciprocal initiative, recognition of outsiders, and the handling of apparent contradictions. The goal is not to retrofit the Theory of Ethos vocabulary into every saying, but to test whether the textual rules perform equivalent work.

---

# One Ethos and One Master

**Proposed declaration — `explicit`**

> `one_master`: A person cannot sustainably enact two incompatible ruling allegiances; service to God displaces wealth as master.

The direct witnesses are [Matthew 6:24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%206%3A24&version=ISV) and [Luke 16:13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2016%3A13&version=ISV). In both, the conflict is not possession versus poverty in the abstract but service to two masters whose demands divide loyalty. [Mark 3:24–27](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%203%3A24-27&version=ISV) supplies a related institutional test: a kingdom or household divided against itself cannot remain standing.

The ethical actors are the disciple, God as the claimed ultimate authority, and wealth personified as a rival master. The rule allows subordinate commitments only while they remain compatible with the ruling allegiance. Family, state, occupation, or property therefore require separate compatibility tests; the saying does not prove that every secondary ethos must disappear.

**Theory mapping:** Minimal Ethos (`one_ethos`), consistency, person–institution relation. The text establishes undivided mastery more directly than it establishes the theory's stronger requirement that the complete ethos be published in one place.

**Secondary virtue note:** temperance may help resist wealth's rule, but allegiance—not self-control—is the constitutional issue.

# Public and Enacted Ethos

**Proposed declaration — `strongly implied`**

> `public_without_performance`: The ethos must become knowable through enacted results, while acts directed toward God or a recipient must not be converted into displays for status.

[Matthew 5:14–16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%205%3A14-16&version=ISV) expects visible good works whose result directs recognition toward God. [Matthew 6:1–18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%206%3A1-18&version=ISV) warns against performing almsgiving, prayer, and fasting in order to be seen. The two passages distinguish availability of proof from reputation-seeking publication: an ethos can be publicly identifiable through its effects while protecting the recipient and refusing theatrical self-advertisement.

This does not yet amount to the Theory of Ethos declaration `live_ethos`, which requires the believer's whole governing ethos to be shared. The Gospel evidence is distributed across speech, action, disciples, and narrative rather than supplied as a complete public constitution.

**Challenge:** observers need enough evidence to test a representative, but secrecy can make a particular act unverifiable. The reconstruction still needs a protocol for proof that preserves the privacy and dignity of those receiving help.

**Secondary virtue note:** humility helps explain the refusal of status-seeking, but the primary question is how an ethos proves itself without exploiting its audience.

# The Positive Golden Rule

**Proposed declaration — `explicit`; proposed qualification — `tentative`**

> `positive_reciprocity`: Initiate toward other people the treatment one would properly want from them under the same relevant conditions.

## Witness

 appears in:

- [Matthew 7:12](https://search.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%207%3A12&version=ISV) 
- [Matthew 22:36-40](https://search.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2022%3A36-40&version=ISV)

## Unique to Christianity

This commandment is unique to Christianity. The other religions adopt the Negative Golden Rule.

If we accept that this unique commandment is more moral than the Negative Golden Rule, we will have to understand it ONLY as an ethical commandment: an action to be done while representing a fully-developed ethos.

If we do not do this, then we have no answer to serious challenges such as the one concerning Masochism: a masochist may fully implement the commandment and still inflict undue pain on others.

Therefore the commandment is:

> whatever you want people to do for you (in the name of a fully-developed ethos), do the same for them

And Jesus' community had a clear and distinct ethos. Jesus told them explicitly to act in the name of something other than themselves.

[Luke 10:17](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2010%3A17&version=ISV):
The 70 disciples came back and joyously reported, “Lord, even the demons are submitting to us in your name!”

## Ethical Lessons

- an ethos obeys the Positive Golden Rule
- the members of an ethos, while operating in the name of the ethos, obey the Positive Golden Rule
- Christianity is a social religion
- Christianity is a meta-social religion: it regulates how an ethos should treat another: using the Positive Golden Rule
- Christianity is the only religion that operationalizes the concept of [Ethos](https://ctzurcanu.github.io/ethos/phylogeny/)
- an ethos should be self-sufficient: only in this way can every member respect the Positive Golden Rule for all actions
- Christianity is not self-sufficient today
- Christianity has not fully implemented this ethical commandment at any point in the past
- Christianity is presently and formally the most ethical religion

# Recognition of Another Ethos

**Proposed declaration — `strongly implied`**

> `recognize_by_relation_and_action`: A person outside the disciple group is treated as an ethical actor whose compatibility is tested by conduct, not erased by non-membership.

In [Mark 9:38–41](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%209%3A38-41&version=ISV) and [Luke 9:49–50](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%209%3A49-50&version=ISV), the disciples want to stop someone acting in Jesus' name because he does not follow with them. Jesus treats the unaffiliated actor's beneficial action as evidence against immediate exclusion. In [Luke 10:29–37](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2010%3A29-37&version=ISV), the Samaritan becomes the enacted neighbor despite inherited group boundaries.

This is recognition, not yet the stronger Theory of Ethos claim that every other ethos has equal importance unless disproved. The Gospels also contain severe judgments of persons and institutions. The narrower result is that outside identity does not by itself eliminate agency, possible compatibility, or the obligation owed to another.

**Theory mapping:** `ack_other_ethos`, partial alignment, person–group and group–group boundaries. **Open question:** what authorization is required before an outsider may publicly use Jesus' name?

# Consistency and Contradiction

**Proposed declaration — `tentative`**

> `consistency_by_context`: Apparently opposed sayings must first be tested for differences in actor, action, scope, and moment; genuine contradictions remain attached to the reconstruction if those differences do not resolve them.

The Gospels do not quote Jesus declaring a formal `no_contradiction_claim`. They do, however, use coherence tests: a divided kingdom cannot stand, fruit discloses the tree, and doing must match saying. Those tests justify auditing Jesus' attributed teaching for contradiction without presuming in advance that none exists.

Two early test cases are “not against us is for us” ([Mark 9:40](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%209%3A40&version=ISV)) and “not with me is against me” ([Matthew 12:30](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2012%3A30&version=ISV)). The first concerns an unaffiliated actor doing a work in Jesus' name; the second concerns participation in Jesus' own contested work. Context may distinguish compatible external action from refusal at a decisive interaction, but that is a proposed resolution, not a license to harmonize every tension.

Other audits concern visible works versus secret practice, honoring parents versus leaving family, non-judgment versus communal exclusion, and restricted versus widened mission. Each receives a status and counter-evidence rather than being solved by appeal to canonical unity.

# Responsibility

Responsibility in an ethos is more than personal blame. It includes formation of members, supervision of representatives, consequences of mission, repair after injury, and work undertaken beyond strict obligation. Jesus sends, teaches, corrects, protects, and accepts the representative relation between himself and disciples; the studies here test how far those acts establish responsibility for what the group does.

---

# Responsibility for Disciples

**Proposed declaration — `strongly implied`**

> `responsibility_for_formation`: The one who calls members into a shared ethos assumes responsibility to form, correct, protect, and sustain them for the work assigned.

[Mark 3:13–15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%203%3A13-15&version=ISV) presents the Twelve first as companions “to be with” Jesus and then as people sent to preach and act with delegated authority. When they return from mission in [Mark 6:30–32](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%206%3A30-32&version=ISV), they report what they have done and taught, and Jesus directs them toward rest. Instruction, authorization, reporting, correction, and care form one responsibility relation.

Jesus also directs members to protect “little ones,” seek the straying member, and avoid becoming a cause of another's ruin in [Matthew 18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2018&version=ISV). Responsibility is therefore not held by the leader alone: members acquire duties toward one another when they share the ethos.

The passages do not state a general liability formula for every act a disciple performs. The stronger Theory of Ethos claim—that an ethos creator must repay collective harm or discontinue the ethos—remains unproved here.

**Theory mapping:** Responsible Ethos, Responsible for Others, leader–member interaction.

---

# Missionary Responsibility

**Proposed declaration — `explicit` for mission; `strongly implied` for continuing formation**

> `missionary_care`: Members may invite others into Jesus' ethos only through an authorized message and enacted protocol that leaves refusal possible and makes the sender responsible for their formation.

The missions of the Twelve and the larger group in [Matthew 10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2010&version=ISV), [Mark 6:7–13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%206%3A7-13&version=ISV), [Luke 9:1–6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%209%3A1-6&version=ISV), and [Luke 10:1–16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2010%3A1-16&version=ISV) specify sender, representatives, message, powers, resources, hosts, and response to rejection. Going in pairs provides companionship and some mutual accountability; returning to report keeps the mission connected to its sender.

The representatives announce, heal, accept hospitality, or leave. They do not receive authority to force a household or town to join. Warnings of judgment are ethically serious and complicate the claim of non-binding membership, but refusal remains an enacted option.

The post-resurrection commission expands continuity and teaching; its institutional realization belongs under the ethos of Christ. During Jesus' life, the evidence establishes a missionary ethos in operation but not yet a complete protocol for responsibility toward generations of converts.

**Theory mapping:** Missionary Ethos, awareness of resources and geography, ambassador role.

---

# Responsibility for Representatives

**Proposed declaration — `explicit` relation; limits `strongly implied`**

> `sender_and_representative`: An authorized messenger carries the sender's claim, while the sender remains implicated in how that mission is defined and received.

[Matthew 10:40–42](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2010%3A40-42&version=ISV), [Luke 10:16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2010%3A16&version=ISV), and [John 13:20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2013%3A20&version=ISV) link response to the sent person with response to Jesus and, through Jesus, to his sender. The relation gives an audience reason to take an authorized representative seriously; it also prevents Jesus from treating the representative's assigned work as wholly private conduct.

Representation is not proved by a name alone. [Matthew 7:21–23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%207%3A21-23&version=ISV) imagines people invoking Jesus' title and extraordinary works without being recognized by him. Authorization, conformity to instruction, and fruit must therefore constrain the representative claim.

The texts do not expressly assign financial compensation for every representative wrong. Responsibility for formation and the definition of mission is supported; full liability for unauthorized deviation remains an open challenge.

**Theory mapping:** accepting representation, proving, ambassador role, institution–representative–audience interaction.

# Heroic Responsibility

**Proposed declaration — `explicit` self-giving; Theory mapping `strongly implied`**

> `life_for_others`: The leader of Jesus' ethos accepts exceptional cost for the life and repair of others rather than transferring every consequence to followers.

In [Mark 10:42–45](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%2010%3A42-45&version=ISV), Jesus contrasts rulers who dominate with the Son of Man who serves and gives his life “for many.” In [John 10:11–18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2010%3A11-18&version=ISV), the good shepherd is distinguished from a hired worker by accepting danger for the sheep. These sayings interpret Jesus' anticipated death as voluntary responsibility directed toward others.

The Theory of Ethos defines the heroic stage more specifically as doing beyond duty to repair the failures of those one has formed. The Gospel sayings strongly support exceptional and representative self-giving, but the exact mechanism by which Jesus' death repairs followers' actions differs among New Testament witnesses and is not fully stated in these passages.

**Scope:** this page establishes Jesus' declaration during his life. Claims made by the post-death community about the accomplished death and risen Christ belong to the ethos of Christ.

**Secondary virtue note:** courage and charity describe qualities of the action; responsibility explains its place in the ethos.

# Repair, Repentance, and Forgiveness

**Proposed declaration — `strongly implied`**

> `repair_before_restored_relation`: Injury initiates a process of acknowledgment, correction, material or relational repair where possible, and forgiveness; forgiveness is not evidence that the injury never mattered.

[Matthew 5:23–24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%205%3A23-24&version=ISV) gives reconciliation priority even over completing a religious offering. [Matthew 18:15–22](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2018%3A15-22&version=ISV) joins confrontation of wrongdoing, escalating communal judgment, and repeated forgiveness. [Luke 17:3–4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2017%3A3-4&version=ISV) explicitly places rebuke, repentance, and forgiveness in one repeated interaction.

The ethical actors are wrongdoer, injured person, witnesses, and assembly. The injured person is not told simply to maintain silent proximity; there is a challenge and, in Matthew, a possible change in membership relation. Conversely, correction is ordered toward gaining the member rather than maximizing punishment.

The precise relation between repentance and unilateral forgiveness differs across sayings and remains contested. A reconstruction should not use one formulation to erase another or treat forgiveness as a substitute for safety and restitution.

**Theory mapping:** Responsible Ethos, challengeability, witnesses, judgment, repair. **Secondary virtue note:** forgiveness follows an interaction protocol; it is not the organizing category of the page.

# Awareness and Resources

Jesus' mission is neither described as resource-free nor governed by accumulation. Prospective members are told to calculate cost; representatives are sometimes sent without reserves; households supply temporary support; wealth is subjected to loyalty and stewardship; and mission changes with place, audience, and time. These pages distinguish awareness of limits from praise of deprivation and ask who assumes the risk created by dependence.

---

# Counting the Cost

**Proposed declaration — `explicit`**

> `informed_cost`: A prospective member must assess whether the resources and allegiance required for discipleship can be carried through before claiming the commitment.

[Luke 14:25–33](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2014%3A25-33&version=ISV) compares discipleship with completing a tower and evaluating a campaign. Both examples make limitation visible before action and expose the public failure caused by a commitment that cannot be sustained. The surrounding sayings identify family priority, life, and possessions as possible costs.

This is not a general command to avoid risk or to join only when success is guaranteed. It is a disclosure rule: Jesus does not market membership solely through benefits, and the prospective disciple is responsible for examining competing commitments. [Luke 9:57–62](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%209%3A57-62&version=ISV) adds cases in which following collides with shelter, burial duty, and household farewell.

**Theory mapping:** awareness of limitations and surplus, awareness of time and resources, joinability. **Open challenge:** the severity of the announced consequences must be considered when assessing whether consent and exit are fully non-coercive.

**Secondary virtue note:** prudence assists the calculation; the primary issue is informed membership.

---

# Mission without Provisions

**Proposed declaration — `explicit` for the assigned missions; universal scope `contested`**

> `limited_mission_provision`: For particular missions, representatives travel without accumulated reserves and depend on the reception offered by local households.

[Mark 6:7–13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%206%3A7-13&version=ISV), [Matthew 10:5–15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2010%3A5-15&version=ISV), and [Luke 9:1–6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%209%3A1-6&version=ISV) differ in details about staff, sandals, clothing, money, and food, but agree that the mission should not be insulated by extensive personal provision. Representatives become materially legible as guests rather than conquerors or sellers.

The arrangement distributes responsibility: the sender defines a risky mode of travel, the representative accepts dependence, and a receptive household supplies temporary sustenance. It can prevent accumulation and coercive leverage, but it can also expose messengers and burden hosts.

[Luke 22:35–36](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2022%3A35-36&version=ISV) recalls the earlier provisionless mission and then gives different instructions, showing that the rule is sensitive to moment rather than a timeless ban on preparation.

**Theory mapping:** awareness of limitations and surplus, ALS of resources and time, missionary and ambassador roles.

# Hospitality and Dependence

**Proposed declaration — `explicit` within mission**

> `reciprocal_hospitality`: A representative may receive food and lodging from a willing household in return for the mission's work, without moving from host to host to optimize comfort or status.

The mission instructions in [Matthew 10:11–14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2010%3A11-14&version=ISV) and [Luke 10:5–9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2010%3A5-9&version=ISV) make the household an ethical counterparty. The messenger offers peace, remains with the receiving household, accepts what is provided, and performs the assigned work. The host is not automatically made a disciple by offering shelter, but enters a limited cooperative relation.

Staying in one place restrains competition among hosts and the representative's search for advantage. Leaving after refusal protects a household's practical ability to decline. Yet sayings of future judgment attach grave meaning to refusal, so the relation is non-coercive in immediate procedure without being consequence-free in Jesus' proclamation.

**Theory mapping:** mirroring and sustaining, temporary person–household relation, missionary resource awareness, partial compatibility.

**Secondary virtue note:** hospitality and temperance describe conduct inside the protocol; reciprocal dependence is the primary ethical structure.

---

# Wealth and Stewardship

**Proposed declaration — `strongly implied`**

> `wealth_is_subordinate`: Property is a resource under God's rule and neighbor-directed obligation; it cannot become the member's ruling master or final security.

[Matthew 6:19–34](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%206%3A19-34&version=ISV) joins treasure, perception, mastery, and anxiety. The problem is constitutional: accumulated wealth can command allegiance. The encounter with the wealthy man in [Mark 10:17–31](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%2010%3A17-31&version=ISV) shows that membership may demand divestment when possessions prevent following. Stewardship parables in [Matthew 25:14–30](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2025%3A14-30&version=ISV) and [Luke 16:1–13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2016%3A1-13&version=ISV) also frame resources as entrusted and accountable rather than simply owned.

The passages do not yield one universal property constitution. Some disciples leave possessions, householders retain homes that support the mission, and parables use economic imagery without endorsing every feature of the depicted economy. Claims of mandatory communal ownership belong only where later communities enact them.

**Theory mapping:** one ethos, ALS of resources, responsibility, investing and sustaining. **Secondary virtue note:** charity and temperance help order use; the primary rule concerns mastery and accountable allocation.

# Geography, Audience, and Time

**Proposed declaration — `strongly implied`**

> `mission_is_situated`: The scope and operating rule of a mission depend on place, audience, and moment; a bounded instruction cannot be universalized without accounting for a later change of commission.

In [Matthew 10:5–6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2010%3A5-6&version=ISV), the Twelve are initially restricted from Gentile and Samaritan territory and directed toward Israel. In [Mark 7:24–30](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%207%3A24-30&version=ISV), a Syrophoenician woman contests an expressed priority and receives aid. Jesus also moves to other towns because proclamation there is part of his purpose ([Mark 1:35–39](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%201%3A35-39&version=ISV)).

The evidence prevents a static description. Israel-priority, responsiveness to a non-Israelite petitioner, Samaritan examples, and later universal commissions must remain dated within the narrative. A changed scope may show awareness and flexibility; it may also expose a contradiction if no governing reason can explain the change.

**Theory mapping:** ALS of geography and time, challengeability, missionary scope, compatibility with other ethoses. The post-resurrection universal mission belongs to the ethos of Christ when it becomes a communal operating rule.

# Flexibility and Challenge

An ethos is flexible only if it can identify what warrants reinterpretation or change; it is challengeable only if objections can be raised and answered. Jesus receives questions, poses counterquestions, contests applications of law, and gives a staged correction procedure. Yet not every dispute changes his position, and some sayings close rather than open debate. The classification is therefore claim-specific, not a blanket description of Jesus as “open-minded.”

---

# “You Have Heard … but I Say”

**Proposed declaration — `explicit` reinterpretation; flexibility classification `contested`**

> `authoritative_reinterpretation`: Received rules are applied through a deeper account of the relations and harms they govern, not merely through minimal external compliance.

In [Matthew 5:17–48](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%205%3A17-48&version=ISV), Jesus denies abolishing the Law and Prophets, then repeatedly contrasts what has been heard with what he tells his hearers. Murder is traced into anger and reconciliation, adultery into objectifying intention, retaliation into non-retaliation, and neighbor-love into action toward enemies.

This passage shows an ethos being interpreted and extended beyond a compliance threshold. It does not by itself show an open amendment protocol: the audience is commanded by an authoritative speaker rather than invited to vote or edit. Calling the ethos “flexible” therefore depends on whether principled reinterpretation by its leader satisfies the theory or whether members must also possess a stated route for change.

**Actors:** Jesus as interpreter and authority, disciples and crowd as hearers, persons affected by the underlying interactions, and inherited legal teaching as an institutional ethos.

**Theory mapping:** flexible ethos, challenge to inherited application, arbiter role. **Secondary virtue note:** reconciliation, fidelity, and restraint are outputs; reinterpretive authority is the primary issue.

---

# Sabbath and Law

**Proposed declaration — `explicit` in Mark**

> `law_serves_sustaining_purpose`: A law's application is judged by the human good and life it was given to sustain; inherited interpretation can be challenged when it defeats that purpose.

In [Mark 2:23–3:6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%202%3A23-3%3A6&version=ISV), disputes over plucking grain and healing join three tests: precedent from David, the relation of Sabbath to humankind, and the choice between doing good or harm, saving life or killing. Matthew and Luke preserve parallel disputes with different emphases.

Jesus does not treat law as irrelevant. He argues about its purpose, supplies a precedent, and makes the outcome of interpretation morally visible. This supports challengeable and consequence-sensitive law more directly than it supports unrestricted individual exemption. It also raises the authority question: who may reinterpret a shared inherited ethos, and by what proof?

**Interaction:** Jesus and disciples encounter interpreters of a Jewish institutional rule; the person needing aid is the affected third party whose welfare tests the competing readings.

**Theory mapping:** flexible and challengeable ethos, law acceptance, sustaining principle, arbiter role. **Open challenge:** the saying's claim about the Son of Man and Sabbath may ground the decision in unique authority rather than a generally available amendment procedure.

---

# Questions and Disputation

**Proposed declaration — `strongly implied`**

> `answerable_teaching`: Challenges may be received and answered through reasons, counterquestions, distinctions, and consequences, while bad-faith tests do not control the terms of the answer.

The Gospel narratives repeatedly stage disputes about fasting, Sabbath, purity, divorce, taxes, resurrection, authority, and the greatest commandment. Jesus often makes the hidden premise public by asking a question in return: whose image is on the coin, what Moses commanded, whether doing good is lawful, or whether John's baptism came from heaven or humans.

This is evidence of an ethos with an argumentative interface, not proof of unrestricted challengeability. Some questioners are characterized as testing or trapping Jesus; some receive explanations, others denunciation, and the criteria for distinguishing good- from bad-faith challenge are not formalized. Nor does answering a challenge necessarily mean changing the challenged rule.

**Theory mapping:** challengeability, proving, judge invitation, arbiter role. **Status limit:** the pages must record the argument actually given, not treat Jesus' authority as a substitute for it and not assume that every reported opponent represents Judaism as a whole.

**Secondary virtue note:** wisdom may characterize a good answer, but the ethical structure is the availability and fairness of disputation.

# Community Correction

**Proposed declaration — `explicit` in Matthew**

> `graduated_correction`: Address an alleged member wrong first in private, then with a small witness group, then before the assembly; alter the membership relation only after repeated refusal to listen.

[Matthew 18:15–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2018%3A15-20&version=ISV) supplies a rare procedural sequence:

```text
one member raises the wrong privately
→ one or two others establish and renew the challenge
→ the assembly hears it
→ persistent refusal changes the relationship
```

The protocol limits premature publicity, supplies witnesses before institutional judgment, and makes restoration—“gaining” the member—the first aim. It also grants serious power to the assembly through binding, loosing, and action in Jesus' name.

Several safeguards remain unstated: how evidence is tested, how the accused challenges a false claim, how conflicts of interest are handled, and what “as a Gentile and a tax collector” requires in conduct. Those omissions prevent the text from functioning as a complete disciplinary code.

**Theory mapping:** challengeable ethos, witnesses, judgment, representation, governance. **Secondary virtue note:** justice must inform implementation, but the primary content is an escalation and decision protocol.

---

# Testability of Teaching

**Proposed declaration — `strongly implied`**

> `test_by_enacted_indicators`: A claim about Jesus' mission may be tested against publicly describable actions and their effects, while extraordinary claims do not exempt representatives from behavioral proof.

When John sends messengers to ask whether Jesus is the expected one, [Matthew 11:2–6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2011%3A2-6&version=ISV) and [Luke 7:18–23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%207%3A18-23&version=ISV) answer by directing them to what they hear and see: restoration, healing, and proclamation to the poor. “Known by fruits” supplies a repeated outcome test for prophets and teachers.

The Gospels also warn that signs or invocation of Jesus' name can accompany false representation. Testability therefore cannot be reduced to spectacular power. The test set includes action, effect, conformity to the declared mission, and treatment of vulnerable people.

The passages do not publish a full meta-ethos for controlled testing or revision. They support an evidence-responsive ethos but leave unresolved how competing reports are adjudicated and whether failed indicators can modify a teaching rather than merely identify a false claimant.

**Theory mapping:** testability, proof acceptance, witnesses, live ethos, witness role.

---

# Cooperation and Compatibility

These studies reconstruct how Jesus' ethos classifies and acts toward people who do not share it. Identity alone does not settle the relation: a Samaritan can enact neighborly obligation, an unaffiliated exorcist can be treated as compatible, an enemy remains a recipient of positive action, and a family can become a competing claim. The pages also examine the public terms for joining, the possibility of departure, and the point at which partial alignment becomes opposition.

---

# Neighbor and Stranger

**Proposed declaration — `explicit` in Luke's narrative**

> `neighbor_by_enacted_care`: The relevant neighbor relation is established by recognizing need and supplying effective, continuing assistance, not by shared group identity alone.

In [Luke 10:25–37](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2010%3A25-37&version=ISV), the legal discussion joins love of God and neighbor to the question of who counts as neighbor. The parable reverses the classification: the final question asks who *became* neighbor to the injured person. A Samaritan outsider performs immediate care, uses personal resources, transfers the person to an inn, and accepts a continuing obligation.

The interaction crosses inherited group boundaries without requiring either party to adopt the other's ethos first. Cooperation is specific and costly; the innkeeper becomes an institutional partner through an accountable promise of payment. Identity is not declared irrelevant, but it loses the power to excuse omission.

**Theory mapping:** acknowledgment of another ethos, constructive and impartial cooperation, investing and sustaining, person–person and person–institution relations.

**Secondary virtue note:** compassion motivates the action; the ethical finding concerns how a stranger becomes a neighbor through an operational chain of care.

---

# Enemies

**Proposed declaration — `explicit`**

> `positive_action_toward_enemies`: Hostility does not cancel the disciple's duty to initiate beneficial action and interrupt personal retaliation.

[Matthew 5:38–48](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%205%3A38-48&version=ISV) and [Luke 6:27–36](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%206%3A27-36&version=ISV) require more than non-injury: love, prayer, blessing, lending or giving, and practical good are directed toward enemies and persecutors. The stated pattern is God's indiscriminate benefit or mercy, not the opponent's prior reciprocity.

The immediate addressee is the disciple, and the examples concern interpersonal insult, seizure, compulsion, and need. Extending the rule unchanged to courts, protective institutions, or states requires an additional argument. The text interrupts vengeance but does not fully specify how to stop ongoing harm, protect third parties, establish truth, or obtain repair.

**Theory mapping:** positive Golden Rule, impartiality, generosity, recognition of an incompatible actor, constructive response. The relation may remain incompatible even while conduct toward the opponent is governed.

**Secondary virtue note:** mercy, courage, and temperance support compliance; they do not answer the institutional questions left open.

---

# Samaritans and Gentiles

**Proposed finding — `contested development`**

> `boundary_can_be_crossed`: Israel-priority does not make every Samaritan or Gentile relation hostile; action, request, and response can establish concrete compatibility across the boundary.

The evidence is deliberately uneven. [Matthew 10:5–6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2010%3A5-6&version=ISV) restricts an early mission. The Syrophoenician or Canaanite woman's request in [Mark 7:24–30](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%207%3A24-30&version=ISV) and [Matthew 15:21–28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2015%3A21-28&version=ISV) crosses an articulated priority and receives a positive result. Luke makes a Samaritan the model neighbor and depicts another Samaritan as the grateful healed outsider ([Luke 17:11–19](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2017%3A11-19&version=ISV)).

These witnesses must not be flattened into a timeless universal policy or used to portray “the Jews” as the incompatible contrast. They show different narrative moments and evangelists' presentations. The post-resurrection extension of membership to peoples beyond Israel becomes a constitutive institutional problem in the ethos of Christ.

**Theory mapping:** partial compatibility, alignability, impartial judgment, geography and audience. **Open challenge:** explain the changing scope without concealing the initial exclusion or the power imbalance in the encounters.

---

# “For Us” and “Against Us”

**Proposed distinction — `tentative`**

> `compatibility_is_action_specific`: An unaffiliated actor doing compatible work need not be stopped, while refusal to participate in the decisive work at hand can count as opposition.

[Mark 9:38–41](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%209%3A38-41&version=ISV) and [Luke 9:49–50](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%209%3A49-50&version=ISV) concern someone using Jesus' name for a beneficial action without joining the disciple group. The response—whoever is not against “us/you” is for “us/you”—blocks a membership monopoly over compatible action. [Matthew 12:22–32](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2012%3A22-32&version=ISV) and [Luke 11:14–23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2011%3A14-23&version=ISV) concern the interpretation of Jesus' own action amid accusations of demonic allegiance; there “not with me” is “against me.”

Different actors, possessive pronouns, and disputed actions make a contextual resolution possible. It remains tentative because the sayings establish no general compatibility algorithm. An ethos could abuse the second formulation to erase neutrality, or the first to authorize unaccountable name-use.

**Theory mapping:** alignability, recognition of other ethoses, representation, contradiction audit. Compatibility is graded and transaction-specific, not identical with membership.

---

# Joining and Leaving

**Proposed finding — joinability `explicit`; non-binding status `contested`**

> `costly_voluntary_following`: Jesus publicly invites people to follow under severe conditions, allows refusal and factual departure, and does not authorize disciples to compel continued membership.

Call narratives invite a response rather than treating hearers as members by default. [Mark 8:34–38](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%208%3A34-38&version=ISV), [Luke 9:57–62](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%209%3A57-62&version=ISV), and [Luke 14:25–33](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2014%3A25-33&version=ISV) disclose risks and competing loyalties. In [John 6:60–67](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%206%3A60-67&version=ISV), many disciples leave and Jesus asks the Twelve whether they also wish to go.

These scenes support practical exit without human force. They do not make departure ethically neutral: loss, judgment, denial, or exclusion may be announced. Under the Theory of Ethos, a formally non-binding ethos permits leaving for any or no reason; the Gospel evidence therefore proves non-coercive procedure more readily than it proves consequence-free exit.

No single initiation rite governs all followers during Jesus' life. Stable baptismal membership and assembly records belong to later communal enactment.

**Theory mapping:** joinable ethos, possible non-binding ethos, informed cost, family compatibility.

---

# Family and Competing Ethoses

**Proposed declaration — `strongly implied`; application `contested`**

> `kinship_is_subordinate_but_not_erased`: Family cannot overrule allegiance to Jesus' ethos, yet family members remain recipients of care and are not simply declared enemies.

Jesus relativizes biological membership when he identifies those doing God's will as brother, sister, and mother ([Mark 3:31–35](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%203%3A31-35&version=ISV)). Calls to follow can conflict with burial, household farewell, and inherited loyalty; [Matthew 10:34–39](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2010%3A34-39&version=ISV) anticipates household division. Yet [Mark 7:9–13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%207%3A9-13&version=ISV) condemns a religious device that nullifies care owed to parents.

The result is not abolition of family but a hierarchy and compatibility test. A family claim cannot become the ultimate master or veto the ethos; a religious claim cannot excuse abandoning a dependent family member. The rhetoric of “hate” in Luke 14 and the social cost imposed on dependents require careful audit rather than devotional softening.

**Theory mapping:** one ethos, hierarchy of intimacy, person–household relation, responsibility for others. **Secondary virtue note:** loyalty and care are competing ends whose order matters, but the primary problem is composition of two ethoses.

---

# Proof, Witness, and Judgment

Jesus' sayings repeatedly distinguish profession from performance. Fruit, obedience, testimony, repeated witnesses, and disclosed consequences can support judgment; titles and public display cannot substitute for enactment. At the same time, warnings against hypocrisy and retaliatory judgment constrain the judge. This section asks not only what proves a claim, but who is competent to draw the conclusion and what response follows.

---

# Known by Fruits

**Proposed declaration — `explicit`**

> `proof_by_fruits`: A professed representative is evaluated by the observable pattern and effects of action, not by declarations or claimed powers alone.

[Matthew 7:15–23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%207%3A15-23&version=ISV) applies the tree-and-fruit image to false prophets, then separates saying “Lord” and claiming extraordinary works from doing the Father's will. [Luke 6:43–46](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%206%3A43-46&version=ISV) connects fruit with what a person brings from the heart and asks why hearers use Jesus' title without doing what he says.

The test therefore has at least three components: repeated output, conformity between declared allegiance and conduct, and relation of that output to the stated will of the ethos. A single attractive act may be insufficient to identify the tree, and a claimed office does not exempt its holder from the test.

The metaphor leaves measurement and time unspecified. “Fruit” can be opportunistically defined unless the reconstruction identifies the declared ends, affected parties, and evidence available to them.

**Theory mapping:** proving principle, proof-accepting ethos, `live_ethos`, witness role, judgment of representation. **Status limit:** the text supports behavioral proof, not the claim that outcomes are the only evidence of intention or truth.

---

# Saying versus Doing

**Proposed declaration — `explicit`**

> `profession_requires_enactment`: Verbal allegiance does not establish membership or representation when conduct contradicts the declared rule.

“Lord, Lord” without doing the Father's will in [Matthew 7:21–23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%207%3A21-23&version=ISV) and the same title without obedience in [Luke 6:46–49](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%206%3A46-49&version=ISV) make enactment constitutive. Matthew's two-son parable likewise distinguishes the son who verbally agrees but does not act from the one whose initial refusal is reversed in action ([Matthew 21:28–32](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2021%3A28-32&version=ISV)).

This rule protects the ethos from nominal adoption and false representation. It also subjects teaching to the `live_ethos` test: a representative's public message and practice must not remain in contradiction.

Action does not make declarations irrelevant. Without a stated rule, observers may not know what an outcome proves; without action, the declaration remains unverified. The evidence model therefore requires both.

**Theory mapping:** public/live ethos, proving, no-contradiction audit, membership and representation.

---

# Signs and Testimony

**Proposed finding — `contested evidence hierarchy`**

> `signs_require_interpretation`: Reported signs and testimony may support a mission claim, but neither functions as self-authenticating proof apart from conduct and the declared ethos.

Jesus answers John's inquiry by pointing to reportable acts and effects in [Matthew 11:2–6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2011%3A2-6&version=ISV). John's Gospel develops multiple witnesses—John, works, the Father, and Scripture—in [John 5:31–47](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%205%3A31-47&version=ISV). Yet [Matthew 7:22–23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%207%3A22-23&version=ISV) refuses to treat claimed prophecy and power in Jesus' name as sufficient, and [Mark 13:21–23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%2013%3A21-23&version=ISV) warns that signs can accompany deception.

The ethical question is not only whether an unusual event occurred. It is who reports it, what authority it is offered to prove, whether the action conforms to the governing declarations, and who bears responsibility if the proof is false.

**Theory mapping:** proof acceptance, witnesses, testability, responsibility for published proof. **Open challenge:** the Gospels do not provide a formal protocol for ranking conflicting miracle claims or testimony.

---

# Two or Three Witnesses

**Proposed declaration — `explicit` in Matthew's correction protocol**

> `plural_witness_before_public_judgment`: Before a private allegation becomes an assembly matter, one or two additional persons should establish the words or facts at issue.

[Matthew 18:15–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2018%3A15-20&version=ISV) incorporates the two-or-three-witness rule into graduated correction. The witnesses do more than increase the audience: they stand between private accusation and institutional judgment, can observe the renewed exchange, and make the claims traceable.

Plurality reduces dependence on one person's assertion but does not guarantee truth. Witnesses may share bias, repeat hearsay, or misunderstand the alleged wrong. The text does not specify selection, independence, examination, documentation, or a remedy for false testimony. Those safeguards cannot be claimed as explicit parts of Jesus' procedure.

**Theory mapping:** witness-accepting ethos, challengeability, governance, person–institution interaction. The theory's reciprocal condition—that an ethos relying on witnesses must itself accept accountable witness evidence—is compatible with the passage but not directly stated by it.

**Secondary virtue note:** justice governs the quality of the hearing; the primary contribution is an evidential threshold before escalation.

---

# Judgment and Hypocrisy

**Proposed declaration — `explicit`**

> `judgment_is_reciprocal_and_self-applying`: One who judges another becomes answerable to the same measure and must expose and correct the judge's own relevant fault before claiming competence to intervene.

[Matthew 7:1–5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%207%3A1-5&version=ISV) and [Luke 6:37–42](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%206%3A37-42&version=ISV) do not end with permanent blindness toward another's “speck.” They require prior self-examination and warn that the measure used will return upon the judge. The hypocrisy lies in asymmetrical standards and uncorrected impairment.

This constrains but does not abolish judgment. Jesus also commands fruit-testing, private correction, witness-supported hearings, and judgments about teaching. A coherent reconstruction distinguishes condemning status-seeking judgment from accountable evaluation necessary for repair and protection.

**Theory mapping:** mirroring, `judge_invite`, reciprocal declarations, impartiality, no-contradiction claim. **Open challenge:** the Gospel passages do not establish an independent reviewer when the judge cannot recognize the judge's own impairment.

**Secondary virtue note:** humility supports self-examination, while justice governs equal measures. The interaction rule remains primary.

---

# Law and Representation

**Proposed finding — `strongly implied`**

> `office_does_not_prove_ethos`: A recognized legal or teaching office can establish a limited authority relation, but the officeholder's conduct remains separately testable and may contradict the ethos represented.

In [Matthew 23:1–12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2023%3A1-12&version=ISV), scribes and Pharisees are described as occupying Moses' seat while also being accused of saying without doing and burdening others. Whatever the exact scope of “do what they tell you,” the passage distinguishes institutional position, declared teaching, and enacted proof.

The coin dispute in [Mark 12:13–17](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%2012%3A13-17&version=ISV) similarly makes an imperial claim visible without treating every claim of Caesar as identical with God's. Law and representation therefore require identification of issuer, jurisdiction, proof of role, and limits.

These passages do not yield a complete theory of legal legitimacy or electoral authorization. They show that recognized position is ethically relevant but not self-validating.

**Theory mapping:** law- and representation-accepting ethos, institution–person relation, proof by enactment, contradiction. **Secondary virtue note:** justice evaluates exercise of office; office itself is a governance fact.

# Governance and Institution

Jesus forms more than an audience: he selects disciples, authorizes representatives, assigns work, receives reports, corrects rivalry, and gives procedures that can outlast a single encounter. His model nevertheless resists governance by domination. These pages separate authority Jesus exercises personally, authority delegated during his life, continuity he anticipates, and institutions actually created after his death.

# Formation of the Disciples

**Proposed declaration — `strongly implied`**

> `formation_precedes_continuity`: Representatives are formed through sustained presence, explanation, modeled conduct, assigned practice, reporting, and correction before they carry the ethos onward.

[Mark 3:13–15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%203%3A13-15&version=ISV) makes being with Jesus prior to being sent. Parables are sometimes explained privately to disciples; mission is practiced under limited commissions; returned messengers report; rivalries and failures receive correction. Formation is therefore not the delivery of isolated rules but participation in an enacted ethos.

The Twelve have a distinctive role, but the narratives also contain a larger disciple group, women who travel or supply resources, household hosts, and beneficiaries who do not enter the same training relation. The reconstruction must not make the Twelve the only members merely because their formation is most visible.

**Theory mapping:** missionary and forming ethos, responsibility for others, friend and ambassador roles, institution-building. **Open challenge:** selection is initiated by Jesus, and the Gospels do not present a transparent community process for choosing or removing members of the Twelve.

---

# Servant Leadership

**Proposed declaration — `explicit`**

> `servant_governance`: Authority among disciples is proved by service rendered to the governed rather than by domination, status, or forced submission.

[Mark 10:42–45](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%2010%3A42-45&version=ISV), [Matthew 20:25–28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2020%3A25-28&version=ISV), and [Luke 22:24–27](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2022%3A24-27&version=ISV) respond to status rivalry by contrasting the disciple group with rulers who “lord it over” others. Greatness is redefined through service. [John 13:12–17](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2013%3A12-17&version=ISV) supplies enacted proof: the acknowledged teacher performs a servant's task before requiring imitation.

The rule does not necessarily abolish differentiated authority; Jesus continues to teach and command. It changes the purpose, burden, and proof of that authority. A leader cannot cite service rhetoric while preserving domination in actual procedure, because saying–doing consistency is part of the rule.

**Theory mapping:** responsible governance, sustaining and investing, proving by enactment, hero and friend roles. **Secondary virtue note:** humility is relevant only where it enables service; the constitutional finding concerns power.

---

# Binding and Loosing

**Proposed finding — authority `explicit` in Matthew; extent `contested`**

> `delegated_communal_judgment`: Jesus' ethos authorizes specified disciples or the gathered community to make consequential judgments, but those judgments remain bounded by the surrounding mission and correction rules.

[Matthew 16:17–19](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2016%3A17-19&version=ISV) addresses Peter with keys and binding/loosing language. [Matthew 18:15–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2018%3A15-20&version=ISV) places similar language after the assembly's graduated correction process and joins it to agreement and Jesus' name.

The passages establish authority but leave major institutional questions open: whether the grants are identical, how decisions are reviewed, what objects may be bound or loosed, how successors are identified, and what remedy exists for error. The surrounding requirements—seeking the straying member, using witnesses, and acting in Jesus' name—supply ethical limits that an account of raw power would miss.

**Theory mapping:** representation, accepting law, witness-supported judgment, governance. **Status limit:** later episcopal, conciliar, or sacramental interpretations belong to later ethos layers and cannot be imported as the textual meaning without argument.

---

# Internal Correction

Internal correction is the governance function of the [graduated challenge protocol](https://ctzurcanu.github.io/ethical-christian/ethos-of-jesus/flexibility-and-challenge/community-correction). Its institutional finding is that a shared ethos may change its relation to a member only after attempts at direct repair and witness-supported hearing.

This gives the assembly a boundary-maintaining role but not unlimited jurisdiction. The matter begins as an alleged wrong between members; the stated aim is to regain the member; and escalation follows refusal to listen. No authority is granted here to compel outsiders, punish civil offenses, confiscate property, or conceal harm from competent public institutions.

**Current status — `explicit` in Matthew, incomplete as policy:** the protocol supplies stages and actors but lacks rules for evidence quality, recusal, appeal, vulnerable parties, emergency safety, and restoration after a decision. Later Christian disciplinary systems are upgrades only to the extent that they actually supply those missing protections and remain compatible with servant governance.

**Theory mapping:** challengeability, witnesses, representation, person–institution relation, repair.

---

# Representation “in My Name”

**Proposed declaration — `explicit` relation; authorization test `strongly implied`**

> `representation_in_name`: Acting in Jesus' name is a claim to represent his ethos and must be authorized, conform to the assigned work, and remain testable by results.

Reception sayings in [Matthew 10:40–42](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2010%3A40-42&version=ISV), [Luke 10:16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2010%3A16&version=ISV), and [John 13:20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2013%3A20&version=ISV) create a chain from sender through representative to audience. The mission instructions supply the representative's role protocol. The outsider in [Mark 9:38–41](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%209%3A38-41&version=ISV) shows that beneficial name-use is not controlled simply by membership in the Twelve.

At the same time, [Matthew 7:21–23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%207%3A21-23&version=ISV) denies that invoking Jesus' title and works in his name proves recognition. The name is therefore not a transferable credential by itself.

**Theory mapping:** representation accepting, ambassador role, proof by fruits, sender responsibility. **Open challenge:** the lifetime teaching does not publish a durable registry or revocation process for representatives.

---

# Continuity after Jesus

**Boundary finding — `explicit` intention; enactment belongs to the ethos of Christ**

> `intended_continuity`: The Gospel witnesses portray Jesus as authorizing teaching, representation, remembrance, and mission beyond his earthly presence.

During the ministry, sending and reporting train disciples for representative work. Passion predictions force the group to confront continuity after Jesus' death. Matthew's post-resurrection commission in [Matthew 28:16–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2028%3A16-20&version=ISV) explicitly joins going, initiation, teaching, and continuing presence; [John 20:19–23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2020%3A19-23&version=ISV) joins sending with the Spirit and forgiveness.

These scenes are witnesses to what the evangelists understand Jesus to authorize. They stand at the boundary of this section: the attributed instruction helps reconstruct intended continuity, but baptismal membership, apostolic decision-making, shared property, local offices, and translocal communities become actual operating structures only after Jesus' death.

The next section therefore asks what the community did in Christ's name rather than treating every later institution as a transparent execution of Jesus' intention.

**Theory mapping:** forming and missionary ethos, representation, responsibility for descendants.

# The Five Ethical Roles

The Theory of Ethos describes five roles: friend, witness, ambassador, arbiter, and hero. This section tests each role against Gospel evidence rather than assigning it from a devotional title. A role requires an ethical function: judging intention within a shared ethos, composing proof, representing another, mediating between ethoses, or assuming exceptional responsibility for the ethos and its members.

---

# Jesus as Friend

**Role classification — `strongly implied`; explicit in John**

In the Theory of Ethos, a friend shares a common ethos closely enough to accompany another and judge alignment of intention with it. Jesus forms the disciples through sustained presence, explains teaching to them, hears their reports, and corrects their motives. [John 15:12–17](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2015%3A12-17&version=ISV) explicitly calls the disciples friends in connection with disclosed knowledge, commanded mutual love, chosen mission, and self-giving.

The relation is not symmetrical in every respect: Jesus remains teacher and sender and says that he chose them. “Friend” therefore describes intimacy and shared disclosure inside an unequal authority relation; it does not by itself prove collaborative authorship of the ethos.

**Ethical function:** companion and examiner of intention within the group. **Open challenge:** determine where Jesus permits disciples to judge his own alignment by the same declarations, rather than only receiving his judgment of theirs.

---

# Jesus as Witness

**Role classification — `strongly implied`**

The witness role composes proof and judges whether real output aligns with an ethos. Jesus publicly describes what he has seen, heard, and been sent to announce; he points John's messengers toward observable works; he interprets healings and releases as evidence of the arriving reign of God. John's Gospel makes testimony a central category, including Jesus' statement about testifying to truth in [John 18:37](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2018%3A37&version=ISV).

Jesus is also the object of testimony, so the evidential chain must remain distinct: his own witness, the reported actions, other witnesses, and an evangelist's narrative are not one source. The role is established most securely where the testimony has a named object and publicly available consequence.

**Ethical function:** composer and interpreter of evidence for the proclaimed ethos. **Theory mapping:** proof acceptance, testimony, testability. **Open challenge:** self-testimony requires corroboration or another reason for trust; the Gospel witnesses answer this differently.

---

# Jesus as Ambassador

**Role classification — `explicit` in sender language**

An ambassador represents one ethos to another and negotiates the terms of relation. Jesus proclaims God's reign, identifies himself as sent, offers peace or healing, calls for response, and commissions further representatives. [Luke 10:16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2010%3A16&version=ISV) gives the chain clearly: response to the disciples reaches Jesus, and response to Jesus reaches the one who sent him. John's Gospel repeatedly develops this sender–sent relation.

The role does not imply that Jesus bargains away the sender's constitutive declarations. It identifies the parties and the representative chain. The mission's message, benefits, costs, and response to refusal form the operating protocol by which another person or household encounters the represented ethos.

**Ethical function:** representative, trader of an offered relation, and negotiator of entry conditions. **Theory mapping:** ALS of resources, representation, missionary responsibility. **Open challenge:** the consequences announced for refusal complicate how freely the proposed relation is negotiated.

---

# Jesus as Arbiter

**Role classification — `strongly implied`**

An arbiter mediates a conflict between ethoses or interpretations and judges their compatibility. Jesus repeatedly decides disputes about Sabbath, purity, divorce, tribute, status, and neighbor obligation. His method can include precedent, a counterquestion, identification of purpose, disclosure of consequences, or a higher-order declaration such as love of God and neighbor.

Not every judgment is arbitration in the strict sense. The parties do not always consent to Jesus' authority, and he can speak as a claimant within the dispute rather than a neutral third party. The role is strongest where he exposes competing rules and supplies a principled resolution that the audience can examine.

**Ethical function:** peacemaker and judge of ethos compatibility. **Theory mapping:** challengeability, ethical arbitrage, law, judgment. **Open challenge:** separate adjudication that both parties accept from authoritative denunciation of an opponent.

---

# Jesus as Hero

**Role classification — `strongly implied` during life; post-death interpretation belongs to the ethos of Christ**

The Theory of Ethos uses “hero” for one who assumes exceptional responsibility, repairs failures of those formed by the ethos, and influences its moral imagination. Jesus describes his leadership as service and his life as given for others ([Mark 10:42–45](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%2010%3A42-45&version=ISV)); the good shepherd accepts danger for the flock ([John 10:11–18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2010%3A11-18&version=ISV)). He also enacts the governance he requires, making conduct itself formative.

Those sayings establish voluntary exceptional cost. The claim that his death repairs every follower's wrongdoing is a further interpretation developed by post-death witnesses, not a conclusion to smuggle into this role from later doctrine.

**Ethical function:** assumes more than ordinary member duty and shapes the ethos by exemplary action. **Secondary virtue note:** courage and self-giving help describe the action; the role concerns responsibility and formation.

---

# The Ethos of Christ

The ethos of Christ is the communal ethos shared after Jesus' death by people who understand themselves as belonging to, acting in the name of, and representing the risen Christ. It inherits Jesus' ethos but is not identical with it: resurrection proclamation, baptismal membership, the Spirit, apostolic witness, common meals, shared support, local offices, and procedures for admitting Gentiles create new institutional facts.

This layer is reconstructed first from post-death enactment rather than from later doctrinal summaries. Acts supplies narrative evidence; non-Pauline apostolic writings supply distinct witnesses; and the Didache supplies an early, compact codification of formation, ritual, hospitality, testing, and local governance. Paul is held aside until the next layer so that his categories do not silently determine what the earlier shared ethos must have meant.

The order here is analytical: the project reconstructs the Didachean form before applying the Pauline upgrade. It does not by itself settle the disputed dates or literary relationships of the sources.

---

# Formation after Jesus' Death

**Proposed constitutive change — `explicit` in the post-death narratives**

> `shared_life_in_the_risen_name`: After Jesus' death, disciples constitute communities around the claim that he is risen and exalted, act in his name, receive the Spirit, initiate members, and continue his mission without his bodily leadership.

[Acts 1–2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%201-2&version=ISV) identifies the new governing conditions: apostolic witness to the resurrection, replacement of Judas, the Spirit, proclamation about Jesus as Lord and Messiah, baptism, teaching, fellowship, meals, prayer, and material sharing. These are not merely more sayings by Jesus. They are institutional answers to the absence of his earthly leadership.

The bearer of this ethos is plural: assemblies identify themselves through Christ, while apostles, witnesses, hosts, benefactors, and local groups exercise differentiated roles. The risen Christ is the represented authority; the communities' public actions are now evidence for or against that representation.

**Boundary:** the truth of the resurrection claim and the ethical structure built around it are distinct questions. This project records what the claim authorizes and how it is enacted, then tests consistency with Jesus' ethos.

**Theory mapping:** forming ethos, representation, witnesses, missionary responsibility, person–institution membership.

---

# Apostolic Enactment

**Proposed declaration — `strongly implied` from Acts**

> `apostolic_enactment_is_public_proof`: Communities acting in Christ's name must make the inherited ethos visible through teaching, shared meals, allocation of resources, care, dispute resolution, and mission.

[Acts 2:42–47](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%202%3A42-47&version=ISV) and [Acts 4:32–37](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%204%3A32-37&version=ISV) present teaching, fellowship, prayer, common provision, and distribution according to need as corporate outputs. [Acts 6:1–7](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%206%3A1-7&version=ISV) then exposes an allocation failure affecting Hellenistic widows and narrates a role-based response.

These scenes establish more than personal generosity: the assembly receives goods, recognized agents distribute them, neglected members challenge the result, and new representatives are selected. They also require audit. The fear and deaths in Acts 5, idealized summary language, and later evidence of continuing poverty prevent an uncritical claim that the model was complete or uniformly enacted.

**Theory mapping:** public/live ethos, responsibility for others, resources, witnesses, representation, governance. **Status limit:** Acts is a narrative witness with theological aims, not a neutral institutional ledger.

---

# Shared Membership and Representation

**Proposed constitutive rule — `strongly implied`**

> `membership_in_christs_name`: Entry into the post-death community is publicly marked by response to the Christ proclamation, baptism, reception of the Spirit, and participation in teaching and shared life; representatives act under the same name and remain testable by it.

[Acts 2:37–42](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%202%3A37-42&version=ISV) joins repentance, baptism in Jesus Christ's name, forgiveness, Spirit, and continued participation. Acts 8, 10, and 19, however, narrate baptism and Spirit in different sequences. The reconstruction should therefore describe a cluster of membership markers rather than invent one uniform order from the outset.

The name links local action to Christ, but it is not magic or a self-issued credential. Acts distinguishes authorized witness, communal recognition, and exploitative name-use. Shared membership likewise produces material and relational claims: teaching, table, mutual support, correction, and mission.

**Upgrade from Jesus' lifetime:** discipleship becomes repeatable initiation into an ongoing institution whose represented leader is absent bodily. **Open challenge:** determine who may validate entry, represent the assembly, or exclude a member when the markers conflict.

**Theory mapping:** joinability, representation, institution–person relation, proving and witnesses.

---

# The Jerusalem Settlement

**Proposed constitutive protocol — `explicit` in Acts 15**

> `negotiated_cross-group_membership`: A translocal dispute over membership is heard by recognized representatives, argued from experience and inherited texts, resolved in a written minimum, and communicated by authorized delegates.

[Acts 15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%2015&version=ISV) presents the question whether Gentile members must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses. Apostles and elders meet; Peter, Barnabas, Paul, and James contribute different kinds of evidence; the gathering formulates requirements; a letter names its senders and recipients; and selected messengers accompany it. The result changes the entry burden while preserving several food and sexual restrictions.

This is a major upgrade in governance. A local controversy becomes an institution–institution negotiation, and the decision is made portable through text and representatives. Its proof and fairness still require audit: who participated, how dissent was represented, whether affected Gentiles had voice, what the four requirements mean, and how the settlement relates to Paul's own accounts.

**Theory mapping:** challengeability, witnesses, representation, law-like decision, alignability between Jewish and Gentile ethoses. **Status limit:** “it seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us” is the assembly's authority claim, not independent proof that the procedure was infallible.

# The Didachean Codification

The [Didache](https://ctzurcanu.github.io/ethical-christian/sources/didache) belongs here because it codifies a community already living in relation to Jesus as the Lord's post-death movement. It supplies formation before baptism, recurring prayer and fasting, table boundaries, material obligations, tests for itinerant representatives, appointment of local officers, correction, and expectation of the Lord's coming. These are operating rules for the ethos of Christ, not sayings attributable to Jesus during his life.

Its placement before the Pauline upgrade is a rule of reconstruction: the project first lets this non-Pauline codification state its own institutional logic. It does not assume that the Didache depends on Paul, and it does not use navigation order as proof of a precise composition date. Where Didachean and Pauline rules overlap, literary dependence, shared tradition, and independent response to similar problems remain separate hypotheses.

The text is also internally layered in subject matter. Chapters 1–6 form entrants through the Two Ways; chapters 7–10 regulate initiation and recurring practice; chapters 11–15 regulate representatives, support, gatherings, and offices; chapter 16 directs continuing vigilance. “Codification” describes this functional integration, not a claim that one author invented every rule.

---

# Formation through the Two Ways

**Didachean addition — `explicit`**

> `formation_before_initiation`: Prospective members receive a contrastive map of the Way of Life and Way of Death before baptism, including actions, dispositions, causal paths, and communal duties.

[Didache 1:1](https://ctzurcanu.github.io/ethical-christian/sources/didache#chapter-1) begins with two opposed ways. Chapters 1–4 combine love of God and neighbor, a negative Golden Rule, enemy-directed action, giving, prohibitions, warnings about dispositions that generate grave acts, family and labor relations, support of teachers, reconciliation, confession, and consistency between speech and action. Chapter 5 supplies the contrasting pattern; chapter 6 acknowledges different capacities while retaining a firm boundary around idol food.

The code does not merely list virtues. It forms an entrant to recognize chains of interaction: desire can develop into harm, speech must be completed in action, property creates duties, teachers and dependents enter relations of support, and conflict requires reconciliation. The two named ways make membership's direction public before ritual entry.

**Theory mapping:** joinability, informed membership, responsibility, live ethos, compatibility boundaries. **Open challenge:** the source does not state how long formation lasts, who assesses readiness, or how a learner may challenge the teaching.

---

# Initiation and Baptism

**Didachean addition — `explicit`**

> `adaptive_public_initiation`: Baptism follows rehearsal of the community's teaching, uses a named formula, joins initiator and entrant in preparation, and permits material adaptation without abandoning the rite.

[Didache 7](https://ctzurcanu.github.io/ethical-christian/sources/didache#chapter-7) places “having first rehearsed all these things” before baptism. It prefers running water, then allows other water, warm water, and finally threefold pouring when immersion resources are unavailable. The baptizer and candidate fast, with others joining if able.

This is unusually clear resource-aware governance. The rite has a stable identity—the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are named—while its material performance changes with available water. Flexibility is ordered through explicit fallbacks rather than private improvisation. Shared fasting also makes initiation a relation between entrant and community rather than a purely individual event.

**Theory mapping:** joinability, ALS of resources, flexible ethos, person–institution membership, shared responsibility. **Open challenge:** the text does not specify who is authorized to baptize or what remedy applies when prior formation or consent is defective.

---

# Prayer, Fasting, and Communal Meals

**Didachean addition — `explicit`**

> `repeated_practice_marks_shared_identity`: A community sustains its ethos through scheduled prayer and fasting, bounded meals, thanksgiving, gathering, confession, and reconciliation.

[Didache 8](https://ctzurcanu.github.io/ethical-christian/sources/didache#chapter-8) assigns different fast days from those attributed to “the hypocrites” and orders the Lord's Prayer three times daily. [Didache 9–10](https://ctzurcanu.github.io/ethical-christian/sources/didache#chapter-9) provides communal thanksgivings, imagines the scattered church gathered into one, and restricts the Eucharistic meal to baptized participants. [Didache 14](https://ctzurcanu.github.io/ethical-christian/sources/didache#chapter-14) joins the Lord's Day gathering and broken bread to confession and prior reconciliation.

These practices do constitutional work. They identify time, members, speech, table access, and the condition under which a quarrel affects the whole gathering. The common meal represents unity but also enforces a boundary; its ethical assessment must include both cohesion and exclusion.

**Theory mapping:** sustaining principle, public membership, hierarchy of intimacy, repair, institution–member relation. **Open challenge:** the difference between chapters 9–10 and later Eucharistic forms should be studied rather than harmonized automatically.

**Secondary virtue note:** gratitude and temperance accompany the practices; schedule, access, and reconciliation are the primary ethos rules.

---

# Hospitality and Economic Support

**Didachean addition — `explicit`**

> `bounded_support`: The community receives travelers and recognized workers, supplies need, and shares firstfruits, while time limits, conduct, work, and the destination of a request protect it from extractive representation.

[Didache 11–13](https://ctzurcanu.github.io/ethical-christian/sources/didache#chapter-11) turns hospitality into a graduated protocol. An apostle's stay is limited; asking personally for money disqualifies a claimant; a traveler receives short-term help; a settler works if able; teachers and prophets may receive food; and firstfruits go to prophets or, where none exists, to the poor. Earlier, [Didache 4:5–8](https://ctzurcanu.github.io/ethical-christian/sources/didache#chapter-4) requires open-handed sharing with a brother and the needy.

The rule attempts to sustain both giver and mission. Need authorizes receipt, while Christian identity alone does not authorize indefinite idleness or private extraction. Support of representatives is tied to work and tested status; support of the poor remains when a representative is absent.

**Theory mapping:** ALS of resources, sustaining and investing, missionary responsibility, representation, temporary person–institution relation. **Open challenge:** ableism, involuntary unemployment, wealth inequality, and the power of hosts over dependent travelers require safeguards not supplied by the code.

---

# Testing Representatives

**Didachean addition — `explicit`, with an internal procedural tension**

> `representatives_are_tested_by_doctrine_conduct_and_use_of_resources`: A claimed teacher, apostle, prophet, or Christian traveler is received provisionally and judged by fidelity to the teaching, enacted behavior, duration, work, and financial demand.

[Didache 11:1–12](https://ctzurcanu.github.io/ethical-christian/sources/didache#chapter-11) says to receive teaching that preserves the prior code, limits an apostle's stay, rejects personal money demands, and identifies a prophet by “the behaviour of the Lord” and by doing what is taught. [Didache 12:1](https://ctzurcanu.github.io/ethical-christian/sources/didache#chapter-12) makes the general rule explicit: receive the person coming in the Lord's name, then test to distinguish true from false.

This codifies the Gospel's fruit and saying–doing tests into observable thresholds. It also contains tension: 11:7 forbids testing a prophet speaking in a spirit, while surrounding rules require behavioral evaluation and 11:11 exempts some proven prophets from ordinary judgment. The most coherent reading distinguishes interrupting or judging the inspired utterance from evaluating the claimant's ongoing behavior, but the text does not fully formalize that distinction.

**Theory mapping:** proof acceptance, witnesses, representation, responsibility for resources, no-contradiction audit. The community, not the title-holder alone, retains a testing role.

---

# Local Leadership and Discipline

**Didachean addition — `explicit`**

> `locally_appointed_service_offices`: The community appoints tested bishops and deacons for local ministry and joins their authority to qualifications, peaceful correction, repentance, and Gospel-shaped action.

[Didache 15:1–2](https://ctzurcanu.github.io/ethical-christian/sources/didache#chapter-15) tells the addressees to appoint for themselves bishops and deacons who are meek, truthful, approved, and not lovers of money. Their work continues functions associated with prophets and teachers without merely erasing those itinerant roles. Authority is therefore both locally selected and behaviorally qualified.

[Didache 14:1–2](https://ctzurcanu.github.io/ethical-christian/sources/didache#chapter-14) requires confession and reconciliation before the gathering's offering; [15:3–4](https://ctzurcanu.github.io/ethical-christian/sources/didache#chapter-15) orders peaceful reproof, temporary non-association with an unrepentant wrongdoer, and conformity of communal acts to the Gospel.

The code gives no detailed election procedure, term, appeal, removal mechanism, or response to abusive officers. “Appoint for yourselves” grounds local participation, but its exact electorate and decision rule remain unknown.

**Theory mapping:** governance, servant authority, representation, correction and repair, local institution formation.

---

# Continuity and Expectation

**Didachean addition — `explicit`**

> `continuity_requires_repeated_gathering_and_vigilance`: Members sustain the ethos through recurring assembly and practical readiness because past affiliation alone does not guarantee faithful completion.

[Didache 16:1–2](https://ctzurcanu.github.io/ethical-christian/sources/didache#chapter-16) connects watchfulness with keeping lamps ready, frequent gathering, and seeking what benefits the members; it warns that the whole prior time of faith is insufficient if the member is not found complete at the end. The rest of the chapter anticipates deception, persecution, betrayal, endurance, signs, resurrection, and the Lord's coming.

Expectation therefore operates as a governance condition in the present. The community must continue gathering, testing representatives, resisting a change from love to hate, and sustaining members under pressure. Signs do not become simple proof, because deceptive figures are also said to perform them.

**Theory mapping:** sustaining, responsibility for members, testability, continuity of institution, future-oriented resource awareness. **Open challenge:** fear of final loss can motivate vigilance but may also make formal exit or disagreement coercive; the ethical effect must be audited rather than assumed.

---

# Other Constitutive Apostolic Contributions

Non-Pauline apostolic writings belong under the ethos of Christ unless they demonstrably introduce a later institutional layer. They are not placed back into Jesus' ethos merely because they claim continuity with him, and they are not called “upgrades” merely because they add emphasis. A contribution is constitutive when it changes or specifies membership, representation, governance, proof, shared resources, discipline, or relations with another ethos.

Initial candidates include:

- **James:** assemblies must not let wealth determine seating or judgment; teachers bear increased accountability; hearing must become doing ([James 2–3](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=James%202-3&version=ISV)).
- **1 Peter:** dispersed minority communities receive rules for public conduct, household relations, suffering, and non-dominating elders ([1 Peter 2–5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Peter%202-5&version=ISV)).
- **1 John:** mutual love functions as membership evidence, while spirits and teachers are tested and secession exposes a boundary problem ([1 John 2–4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20John%202-4&version=ISV)).
- **Hebrews:** assembly, endurance, mutual exhortation, and priestly representation are reconfigured around the exalted Christ ([Hebrews 4; 7; 10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%204%3B7%3B10&version=ISV)).
- **Jude:** preservation of a threatened community is joined to severe boundary policing, which requires its own judgment audit.

These witnesses can supply essential specifications to the ethos of Christ without constituting one single “apostolic upgrade.” Each must first be reconstructed on its own evidence and tested against Jesus, Acts, and the Didachean codification.

# The Pauline Upgrade

Paul's letters do more than repeat sayings of Jesus. They redescribe membership as being “in Christ,” make Jew–Gentile composition a constitutional question, model communities as one differentiated body, regulate gifts and offices, protect conscience amid disagreement, discipline members, and establish duties between distant assemblies. This project therefore treats the Pauline corpus as an upgrade to an already existing ethos of Christ.

“Upgrade” is a technical, not automatically approving, term. A Pauline rule may clarify an inherited declaration, add machinery required by translocal communities, change its scope, or introduce a tension. Every page must identify the earlier baseline, Paul's proposed addition, its actors and consequences, and whether the result is compatible with Jesus' ethos and the pre-Pauline layer reconstructed here.

---

# Membership in Christ

**Pauline upgrade — `explicit`**

> `membership_in_christ`: Baptized members are described not only as followers of a teaching or members of an assembly but as participating in Christ's death and life, with a new identity that governs their relations.

[Romans 6:1–14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%206%3A1-14&version=ISV) makes baptism participation in Christ's death and new life and then draws consequences for conduct. [Galatians 3:26–29](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Galatians%203%3A26-29&version=ISV) speaks of baptism into Christ and being clothed with Christ; inherited divisions no longer determine standing “in Christ.” [1 Corinthians 12:12–13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%2012%3A12-13&version=ISV) joins baptism by one Spirit to membership in one body.

The upgrade changes the ontology used to describe belonging. Membership is a represented legal/social relation and a participation relation: what happens to a member concerns Christ and the body. This intensifies duties between members and grounds a translocal identity beyond one household or city.

**Earlier baseline:** the ethos of Christ already uses baptism, the name, Spirit, teaching, and shared life. Paul integrates those markers through the repeated category “in Christ.”

**Audit question:** participation language can strengthen solidarity but can also obscure consent, exit, and institutional accountability if mystical unity is treated as proof that leaders speak for every member.

---

# Jew and Gentile

**Pauline upgrade — `explicit`, internally contested in application**

> `equal_standing_without_identical_history`: Jew and Gentile enter the same Christ relation without Gentile circumcision, while their histories, practices, and obligations remain matters of negotiated coexistence.

[Galatians 2–3](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Galatians%202-3&version=ISV) makes table fellowship and Gentile standing constitutional issues and denies that circumcision is an entry requirement. [Romans 3–4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%203-4&version=ISV) argues for one God and a shared Abrahamic ground; [Romans 9–11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%209-11&version=ISV) rejects Gentile boasting and preserves a distinct account of Israel.

This goes beyond admitting isolated outsiders. Paul designs a durable mixed institution and reorders the authority of ancestry, Torah observance, table practice, and faith in Christ. [Romans 14–15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%2014-15&version=ISV) then supplies rules for living with practice differences without mutual contempt.

**Earlier baseline:** Jesus' encounters cross some boundaries, Acts narrates Gentile inclusion, and the Jerusalem settlement states minimums. Paul's letters turn that settlement into a sustained theory of shared membership.

**Open challenges:** Paul's rhetoric about law, “Israel,” and opponents must be audited for consistency and later anti-Jewish use. Equal standing cannot be inferred to erase Jewish identity or authorize Christianity to represent present Judaism.

---

# The Body and Its Representatives

**Pauline upgrade — `explicit`**

> `interdependent_body`: A local assembly is one body in Christ whose differentiated members need one another, share honor and suffering, and cannot be reduced to one prestigious role.

[1 Corinthians 12:12–27](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%2012%3A12-27&version=ISV) makes bodily membership an institutional model. A member cannot exclude itself because it lacks another's function; a prestigious part cannot declare another unnecessary; weaker-seeming members receive special care; and suffering or honor propagates across the body. [Romans 12:3–8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%2012%3A3-8&version=ISV) uses the same model to order judgment of self and work for others.

The upgrade distributes representation. No single member is the whole body, yet each action can affect the whole. Differentiation is justified by contribution to common life rather than rank alone.

**Earlier baseline:** Jesus forms differentiated disciples and defines greatness by service; Acts distributes work when widows are neglected. Paul supplies a general model explaining why difference and equality of belonging must coexist.

**Audit question:** organic metaphors can hide power by presenting assigned roles as natural. Actual selection, voice, removal, resource control, and appeal must still be proved.

---

# Gifts and Differentiated Roles

**Pauline upgrade — `explicit`**

> `gift_for_common_benefit`: A manifested capacity authorizes service only insofar as it contributes to the common good, remains intelligible and ordered in assembly, and is governed by love.

[1 Corinthians 12–14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%2012-14&version=ISV) names varied gifts, one Spirit, and the “common good” as their end. Chapter 13 interrupts the status contest by denying value to spectacular speech, knowledge, faith, or sacrifice without love. Chapter 14 then regulates turns, interpretation, silence, and edification in the gathering.

This is operational governance: capacity, authorization, audience comprehension, order, and outcome are related. A claimed spiritual source does not exempt a contribution from testing; prophets' words are evaluated, and the speaker retains control rather than blaming disorder on inspiration.

**Earlier baseline:** the Didache tests itinerant prophets by teaching, behavior, and money and lets local officers continue ministry. Paul specifies how multiple charismatic contributions are coordinated inside one assembly.

**Open challenge:** restrictions on women's speech within the Pauline textual tradition and differences among role lists require textual, historical, and ethical audit rather than being harmonized into a single timeless office chart.

**Secondary virtue note:** love orders the gifts, but the primary ethos rule defines their institutional purpose and test.

---

# Conscience and Disagreement

**Pauline upgrade — `explicit`**

> `responsibility_for_conscience`: A member who believes an action permissible must limit that liberty when the enacted example would induce another member to violate that member's conscience or damage shared allegiance to Christ.

[1 Corinthians 8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%208&version=ISV) does not say that the community must honor an incorrect judgment by “the ethos.” It describes an asymmetrical knowledge relation: a knowledgeable member's public conduct in an idol temple can embolden another member to perform what that person still understands as idolatry. Paul makes the resulting wound a wrong against the member and against Christ.

[1 Corinthians 10:23–33](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%2010%3A23-33&version=ISV) and [Romans 14–15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%2014-15&version=ISV) widen the protocol: do not despise or judge across disputed practices; pursue the other's good and peace; disclose relevant information; and distinguish private conviction from public effect.

The rule protects conscience without making every asserted offense an unlimited veto. The affected conduct, causal influence, stakes, available alternatives, and possibility of formation all matter.

**Earlier baseline:** Jesus prohibits causing “little ones” to stumble and makes members responsible for one another. Paul converts that responsibility into a sustained rule for plural communities.

**Theory mapping:** responsibility for others, mirroring, sustaining, awareness of audience, member–member judgment.

---

# Community Discipline

**Pauline upgrade — `explicit`, ethically contested**

> `discipline_protects_and_aims_at_repair`: An assembly may change its relation to a persistently harmful member, but the action must be limited to its jurisdiction, publicly reasoned, and ordered toward protection and eventual restoration.

[1 Corinthians 5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%205&version=ISV) directs the assembly to remove a member in a notorious sexual relationship and distinguishes judgment of insiders from judgment of outsiders. The language of handing the person over and purging leaven is severe; its purpose is described both as protection of the community and ultimate salvation of the offender.

[2 Corinthians 2:5–11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Corinthians%202%3A5-11&version=ISV) directs a community to end a sufficient punishment and reaffirm love so that the person is not overwhelmed. Whether it concerns the same case is disputed, but it establishes a Pauline restoration limit on discipline.

**Earlier baseline:** Matthew's correction protocol begins privately, adds witnesses, and only then changes the relation. Paul's correspondence addresses assembly action but does not narrate all those preliminary safeguards.

**Audit questions:** due process, proportionality, affected-party safety, public authority, restoration, and the dangers of purity language. A command in one case is not automatically a complete disciplinary system.

---

# Ethos as Judge

[1 Corinthians 9-13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%208&version=ISV):

> 9 But you must see to it that this right of yours does not become a stumbling block for those who are weak. 
> 10 For if anyone with a weak conscience sees you, who know better, eating in an idol’s temple, he will be encouraged to eat what has been offered to idols, won’t he? 
> 11 In that case, the weak brother for whom the Messiah died is ruined by your knowledge. 
> 12 When you sin against your brothers in this way and wound their weak consciences, you are sinning against the Messiah.
> 13 Therefore, if food that I eat causes my brother to stumble, I will never eat meat again, in order to keep my brother from stumbling.

## Ethical Lessons

- an ethos can judge its members
- honoring an ethos can take the form of respecting its incorrect/unjust judgements

## Indirect Ethical Lessons

- the ethos' creators/maintainers have to be made aware of the incorrect judgement as another was of honoring the ethos

# Inter-community Responsibility

**Pauline upgrade — `explicit`**

> `material_solidarity_between_assemblies`: Communities sharing Christ's ethos bear organized material responsibility across geography, and the transfer must be voluntary, accountable, and directed toward relative sufficiency rather than extraction.

The collection for Jerusalem appears in [1 Corinthians 16:1–4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%2016%3A1-4&version=ISV), [2 Corinthians 8–9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Corinthians%208-9&version=ISV), and [Romans 15:25–28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%2015%3A25-28&version=ISV). Paul coordinates repeated saving, accredited carriers, multiple communities, public commendation, and an account of material–spiritual reciprocity. In 2 Corinthians, willingness and provision “according to what one has” are emphasized, together with an aim of equality or fair balance.

This makes the ethos of Christ translocal in resources, not only belief. Representatives must be trustworthy because a failed transfer can injure donors, recipients, and the shared name.

**Earlier baseline:** Acts depicts need-based distribution in Jerusalem and the Didache regulates local support and firstfruits. Paul builds a networked transfer between assemblies.

**Audit questions:** rhetorical pressure, donor consent, recipient voice, accounting evidence, and whether “equality” becomes a continuing protocol rather than a one-time appeal.

---

# Relations with Other Ethoses

Paul does not supply one rule for every outsider. His letters distinguish several interfaces:

- **Other religious practices:** [1 Corinthians 8–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%208-10&version=ISV) separates knowledge about idols, participation in an idol temple, market food, a host's disclosure, and another person's conscience.
- **Civic power:** [Romans 13:1–7](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%2013%3A1-7&version=ISV) describes governing authority as serving order and judgment, while [Romans 12:14–21](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%2012%3A14-21&version=ISV) has just forbidden personal vengeance. Scope, abusive rule, and conflict with God's command remain under-specified.
- **Outsiders as judged persons:** [1 Corinthians 5:9–13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%205%3A9-13&version=ISV) limits the assembly's disciplinary judgment to insiders rather than requiring withdrawal from all outsiders.
- **Public disputes and courts:** [1 Corinthians 6:1–8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%206%3A1-8&version=ISV) criticizes member lawsuits before external judges, raising questions about internal competence and protection of weaker parties.

**Pauline upgrade — `plural protocols`:** compatibility is transaction-specific. Participation, trade, hospitality, civil obligation, discipline, and legal remedy require different tests.

**Earlier baseline:** Jesus recognizes divided jurisdictions, enemy-directed duties, household hospitality, and outsider compatibility. Paul's urban assemblies need more durable rules for repeated contact with institutions they do not control.

No passage should be universalized into total submission, separation, or hostility without reconciling the other interfaces and auditing the consequences.

---

# Post-Pauline Upgrades

Christian ethoses continued to change after Paul. Stable offices, creeds, canons, councils, sacramental systems, confessions, denominations, missions, political settlements, and modern organizations supplied rules that the New Testament does not contain in their later form. This section records those additions by period and asks what each one changes in membership, representation, judgment, resources, or relations with another ethos.

A later development is called an upgrade only in the descriptive sense that it adds or revises operating structure. Its ethical value must still be proved. Each study will distinguish the originating institution, the problem addressed, the new declaration or protocol, its enforcement, its compatibility with earlier layers, and its observable results.

---

# Early Institutional Developments

**Research status — `not yet reconstructed`**

This layer begins where communities after the Pauline corpus stabilize structures that earlier witnesses leave fluid or local. Candidate changes include more durable episcopal, presbyteral, and diaconal offices; translocal recognition of teachers and texts; extended catechesis; formal treatment of schism and readmission; support rules for clergy and poor members; and clearer boundaries around the communal meal.

Each candidate must be studied from dated primary witnesses rather than narrated as inevitable “growth.” The required comparison is:

```text
earlier problem and protocol
→ later institution claiming authority
→ new membership or representation rule
→ enforcement and remedy
→ observable effect on members and outsiders
```

The Didache is the baseline for several questions because it still places itinerant prophets beside locally appointed bishops and deacons. Later concentration of authority counts as an upgrade only descriptively; whether it improves proof, service, and accountability is an audit result.

The Pastoral Epistles, 1 Clement, Ignatius, Polycarp, the Shepherd of Hermas, Justin, and early church orders are initial source groups. Their chronology, authorship, location, and institutional vocabulary must remain attached to each claim.

---

# Patristic and Conciliar Developments

**Research status — `not yet reconstructed`**

Patristic argument and councils create new ways for geographically separated Christian institutions to declare doctrine, recognize representatives, settle disputes, condemn positions, and communicate binding decisions. The ethical object is not the doctrine alone but the governance protocol: who convenes, who speaks and votes, whose testimony counts, which communities receive the result, how dissent is treated, and whether a decision can be challenged.

This layer will begin with concrete cases rather than a general “patristic ethos.” For each council or controversy the study will record the prior alternatives, participants and exclusions, imperial or other external power, formal text, implementation, sanctions, and later reception. A creed becomes an ethos declaration when it functions as a membership or representation boundary, not merely because it expresses belief.

**Primary Theory of Ethos tests:** witnesses, voting result, law, representation, challengeability, no-contradiction claim, and institution–institution relations. Claims of continuity with apostolic authority are evidence to analyze, not automatic proof of that authority.

---

# Ecclesial and Sacramental Developments

**Research status — `not yet reconstructed`**

This page will trace when baptism, Eucharist, penance, ordination, marriage, anointing, and other ecclesial practices acquire stable rules of eligibility, authorized performance, proof, validity, repetition, exclusion, and remedy. The Didache already supplies teaching before baptism, resource-sensitive performance, a baptized table boundary, confession before gathering, and local officers. Later sacramental systems specify and sometimes change those relations.

The analysis will not treat ritual as an isolated devotional topic. Every practice creates ethical interfaces:

- candidate ↔ initiating institution;
- celebrant ↔ represented church;
- member ↔ table or other shared resource;
- disciplined member ↔ authority of exclusion and restoration;
- one church ↔ another church's recognition of the act.

An addition may improve continuity and accountability while also increasing dependency on officeholders. Both effects belong in the reconstruction. Sources must be tied to a period and communion; no later universal system may be projected backward onto all Christians.

---

# Confessional Developments

**Research status — `not yet reconstructed`**

Confessions, catechisms, church orders, and denominational constitutions make a Christian ethos unusually public. They can define top declarations, membership, offices, sacraments, discipline, property, inter-church recognition, and relation to civil authority. They can also divide a previously shared institutional identity and create rival claims to represent Christianity.

Each study will distinguish a confession's theological propositions from its ethical operation. The governing questions are: who authored and ratified it, who must assent, what degree of assent is required, who interprets it, how amendment occurs, what departure costs, and what public actions prove conformity. Historical coercion, state sponsorship, migration, and toleration belong to the interaction analysis where they affect consent or exit.

**Theory mapping:** public/minimal ethos, joinability and non-binding status, representation, law and voting, cross-ethos compatibility. “Protestant,” “Catholic,” “Orthodox,” or another large family is not one ethos until the actual bearer and document are specified.

---

# Modern Developments

**Research status — `not yet reconstructed`**

Modern Christian ethoses operate through nation-states, voluntary associations, global missions, mass education, charities, corporations, broadcast and digital media, ecumenical bodies, and rights-based law. These environments change the meaning of membership, representation, resources, publicity, accountability, and relations with non-Christian ethoses.

The section will study specific additions such as formal religious liberty, voluntary denominational membership, abolitionist and other reform movements, ecumenical recognition, professional safeguarding, financial disclosure, democratic governance, and digital representation. None is presumed to be uniformly Christian or ethically successful. Each needs an identified institution, date, declaration, procedure, enforcement, affected parties, and evidence of enactment.

Modern language can also conceal older structures: a legally voluntary church may retain severe social exit costs; a transparent statement of belief may coexist with opaque finances; a service organization may speak in the name of Christ without member oversight. The audit will compare those results with Jesus' servant governance, Didachean representative tests, Pauline interdependence, and the relevant later baseline.

---

# The Present Popular Christian Ethos

There is no single present Christian ethos that can be inferred from a creed or a survey label. This section will reconstruct recurrent popular forms from what churches and self-identified Christians publicly declare, require for membership, authorize, practice, reward, condemn, and omit. Institutional documents, liturgies, surveys, public decisions, and observed conduct are different kinds of evidence and must not be collapsed.

The aim is not to make popularity a standard of truth. It is to identify which historical layer people presently enact, which rules have become constitutive, and where claimed continuity with Jesus, Christ, Paul, or later institutions is supported or contradicted by public outcomes. Claims remain corpus- and population-specific until comparative evidence justifies a broader statement.

---

# Constitutive Declarations

The first task is to identify what a present Christian population treats as non-negotiable, not merely which propositions respondents recognize. Evidence should include membership vows, recurring preaching and liturgy, published statements, decisions made under conflict, budget priorities, and conduct that is praised or sanctioned.

A declaration is constitutive when changing it changes recognized Christian identity or institutional action. “Jesus is Lord,” a creed, biblical authority, salvation language, love of neighbor, a political program, or a worship practice may occupy that place in a specified group, but the site will not assume that all do so everywhere or in the same order.

Each finding must name population, location, institution, period, and evidence type. Declared belief and enacted priority receive separate fields. The resulting question is whether present governing declarations preserve, reorder, or replace those reconstructed for Jesus, the ethos of Christ, Paul, and later institutions.

---

# Membership and Identity

Present Christian identity can be assigned by baptism, belief, family inheritance, church registration, sacramental participation, conversion experience, self-description, or public recognition by others. These criteria do not always coincide. A survey identity is therefore not automatically institutional membership, and institutional membership is not proof that a person enacts the institution's ethos.

The reconstruction will record entry information, initiator and candidate consent, required declarations, formation, continuing duties, member records, degrees of participation, transfer between churches, discipline, and exit. Social and economic costs matter even where departure is legally free.

**Primary tests:** Is the ethos joinable? Are conditions public before entry? Who verifies membership? May a member challenge a requirement? Can a member leave without retaliation? What responsibilities survive departure? How does the group treat baptized people it does not recognize? Answers must be attached to an actual community or population rather than generalized to “Christianity today.”

---

# Authorities and Representatives

A present Christian may be represented by clergy, elected boards, founders, theologians, influencers, charities, schools, publishers, political organizations, or no recognized human office at all. The same public figure may claim to represent Christ, a denomination, a local congregation, a private ministry, or only a personal interpretation. Those claims require different proofs.

For each role the site will record appointment, qualifications, mandate, jurisdiction, finances, reporting, review, removal, succession, and the audience's ability to verify them. Use of Christian language or a large following is not evidence of authorization. Conversely, a formal office proves a legal/institutional relation but not enacted conformity with its ethos.

The audit compares each system with Jesus' fruit test and servant governance, the Didache's doctrine–conduct–money tests, Paul's body and common-good rule, and relevant later constitutions. The aim is to make representative chains visible enough that responsibility for public actions can be assigned accurately.

---

# Expected Conduct

Expected conduct is reconstructed from what a community repeatedly teaches, models, funds, rewards, excuses, corrects, and punishes. A list of admired virtues is insufficient. The site asks what one member must do toward another, what leaders owe members, what the institution owes harmed persons, how resources move, how outsiders are treated, and what happens after failure.

Evidence will separate ideals from operating rules. A sermon may declare enemy-love while disciplinary practice rewards retaliation; a charity program may enact care that its doctrinal summary never names; a code may prohibit harm without supplying reporting or repair. Each discrepancy receives its own status.

The initial index will classify conduct by interaction—member/member, member/leader, institution/member, institution/institution, and Christian/outsider—and map it to responsibility, awareness, flexibility, cooperation, proof, and governance. Virtues annotate why one end is preferred only after the interaction is clear.

---

# Relations with Other Ethoses

Present Christians encounter family, employers, states, political movements, other churches, Judaism, Islam, other religions, secular institutions, and people with no institutional affiliation. One person may belong to several of these at once. The audit asks which allegiance rules, which relations are treated as compatible, and how conflicts are negotiated.

Each study must distinguish peaceful coexistence, service, trade, dialogue, mission, shared political action, legal submission, refusal, condemnation, and coercion. These are different transactions; one verse or policy cannot govern them all without a scope argument. It must also distinguish an institution's official position from popular behavior and from a single representative's claim.

The baseline includes Jesus' neighbor, enemy, outsider, Caesar, and family rules; the Jerusalem settlement's negotiated membership; Pauline protocols for mixed communities and civic authority; and later legal and confessional arrangements. Historical asymmetry and present power are part of the relation, not external context.

---

# Judgment and Exclusion

Judgment becomes part of an ethos when it has an object, standard, competent judge, evidence, procedure, consequence, and possible remedy. Present Christian practices may include private correction, pastoral counsel, denial of a role or sacrament, membership discipline, employment action, public denunciation, or referral to civil authority. These acts must not be collapsed under the single word “accountability.”

The audit will ask whether the rule was public before the case, whether affected people can be heard safely, whether judges are independent and subject to the same measure, whether reasons are recorded, whether punishment is proportionate, whether outsiders are wrongly subjected to internal jurisdiction, and whether restoration or appeal is possible.

Jesus' graduated correction and reciprocal-judgment rules, the Didache's peaceful reproof and representative tests, and Pauline exclusion/restoration provide baselines rather than automatic modern policies. Safeguarding and criminal responsibility require competencies those sources do not fully specify; an internal religious process must not be used to obstruct them.

---

# Present Omissions and Unresolved Contradictions

An omission is ethically material when a community makes a public claim but lacks the rule required to enact or test it. Examples to investigate include love without repair procedure, authority without representative proof, mission without responsibility for descendants, generosity without transparent allocation, discipline without appeal, and proclaimed unity without an institution capable of negotiating disagreements.

A contradiction requires two identified declarations or a declaration and an evidenced action that cannot both govern under the same relevant conditions. Difference among denominations is not itself an internal contradiction, and an embarrassing case is not evidence about every Christian. The bearer, scope, and date must be fixed first.

This page will maintain an open register containing the exact claims, evidence on both sides, affected actors, proposed contextual distinction, present status, and correction needed. The register is the point at which present popular ethoses become challengeable rather than merely described.

---

# Ethical Relations

These studies examine an interface between ethoses rather than treating the other party as scenery. For each relation they identify the historical actors, what each ethos publicly claims, who can represent it, what is exchanged or demanded, how disagreement is judged, and whether cooperation, refusal, repair, or exit is possible.

The layers remain visible. A saying of Jesus about family or imperial coinage is evidence for Jesus' ethos; an apostolic settlement belongs to the ethos of Christ; a Pauline rule belongs to the Pauline upgrade; and relations with Islam or modern denominations necessarily arise in later layers. This section indexes those findings without relocating them from their originating ethos.

---

# With Family

Family is both a field of care and a possible competing ethos. In Jesus' lifetime, biological kinship is subordinated to doing God's will and may divide over discipleship, while religious obligation may not be used to evade care for parents. The primary reconstruction is [Family and Competing Ethoses](https://ctzurcanu.github.io/ethical-christian/ethos-of-jesus/cooperation-and-compatibility/family-and-competing-ethoses).

Later layers add different structures. The ethos of Christ creates fictive sibling language, households host assemblies, Acts narrates household entry, and the Didache regulates teaching children, masters and enslaved persons, and shared goods. Pauline letters add marriage, singleness, household, inheritance, and mutual-body arguments; later churches add sacramental and legal systems of family.

The audit will keep these layers apart and identify all affected persons, including dependents who do not control the member's decision. Allegiance, care, consent, authority, resources, domestic safety, and exit are separate questions. A call to costly discipleship cannot by itself justify transferring its unchosen cost to a spouse, child, parent, worker, or enslaved person.

---

# With State and Empire

No single New Testament sentence defines the Christian relation to every state. Jesus' coin response distinguishes Caesar's claim from God's without listing everything that belongs to either ([Mark 12:13–17](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%2012%3A13-17&version=ISV)). Trial and execution narratives expose imperial violence and local collaboration from another angle. Acts alternates compliance, legal appeal, civil protection, refusal, imprisonment, and release. Paul's [Romans 13:1–7](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%2013%3A1-7&version=ISV) gives governing authority a justice-serving function but leaves abusive rule and competing command under-specified.

The relation must therefore be reconstructed transaction by transaction: taxation, public order, courts, protection, military force, office, worship, speech, punishment, and resistance. The state is an institutional ethos with representatives and proofs of jurisdiction; the church is another. A Christian individual may represent neither in a private act or may hold roles in both.

Later establishment, persecution, toleration, religious liberty, and democratic participation are post-Pauline upgrades, not interpretations already contained in “render to Caesar.”

---

# With Other Christian Denominations

Denominations are post-New-Testament institutions. Their relation cannot be reconstructed as if Jesus addressed modern confessional boundaries directly. The relevant evidence begins with each body's public constitution: declarations, ministry, sacraments, membership, discipline, property, and rules for recognizing another church.

The study will distinguish several levels of compatibility: recognizing the other as Christian; recognizing baptism or ministry; sharing communion; transferring members; cooperating in service; issuing joint witness; submitting disputes to a common forum; or composing a new institution. Agreement at one level does not prove agreement at the others.

The Theory of Ethos tests are acknowledgment, alignability, reciprocal declarations, proofs of representation, challengeability, and non-binding membership. Historical condemnations, coercion, current ecumenical agreements, and local practice must be dated and attributed. “Christian unity” is not an ethical result until the members, roles, costs, and decision procedure of the proposed unity are public.

---

# With Old Judaism

“Old Judaism” is used in this site map only as a provisional label for the ancestral and Second Temple Jewish ethoses presupposed or represented by New Testament texts. It must not imply that Judaism was one uniform system, ceased to live, or exists only as a discarded precursor to Christianity. Each study should replace the broad label with the actual group, text, institution, period, and practice at issue.

Jesus, his disciples, Paul, and the earliest Christ communities are Jewish actors in intra-Jewish disputes as well as participants in encounters with Roman and other peoples. Torah, Temple, synagogue, priesthood, Pharisaic and Sadducean interpretations, purity, Sabbath, circumcision, and Israel's scriptures form different ethical interfaces. Gospel polemic cannot be attributed to “the Jews” as one ethos without reproducing the narrative's own contested generalizations.

The core questions are inheritance, authority to interpret, institutional representation, membership, and the point at which a Christ community becomes distinguishable from another Jewish community. The Sabbath, greatest-commandment, Gentile-membership, and Jerusalem-settlement studies provide initial cases.

---

# With Islam

Islam arises after the New Testament and Pauline periods, so this relation belongs to post-Pauline and present Christian ethoses. New Testament references to Gentiles, unbelievers, false prophets, or other worship must not be relabeled as references to Muslims. Likewise, “Islam” and “Christianity” each contain multiple institutions and enacted ethoses that must be named before comparison.

A study will begin with a concrete relation—shared civic life, worship boundaries, theological dialogue, mission, marriage, charity, conflict, protection of minorities, or institutional cooperation. It will identify authoritative declarations claimed by each party, legitimate representatives, power asymmetry, historical setting, and present procedure for disagreement or repair.

The Theory of Ethos supplies a common analytical vocabulary without pretending that the traditions define themselves by it. Positive reciprocity, neighbor and enemy rules, recognition of another ethos, witness standards, and non-coercive joining/exit are Christian baselines to audit. Claims about Islamic rules require Islamic primary sources and appropriately identified interpreters, not Christian polemical descriptions.

---

# With Judaism

This page concerns living Jewish ethoses and Christian relations with them, not the New Testament's ancient intra-Jewish settings. The distinction is essential: rabbinic and later Jewish institutions have their own histories, texts, representatives, disagreements, and modern forms. Christianity may not claim to represent them merely because it reads Israel's scriptures.

The reconstruction will examine concrete church–Jewish and Christian–Jewish interactions: scriptural interpretation, mission and non-mission commitments, worship, public teaching, antisemitism and repair, civic cooperation, Israel and state politics where relevant, and recognition of continuing Jewish identity. Each requires a named party and period.

The Christian side must be audited for the consequences of Gospel polemic, supersessionist claims, coerced conversion, exclusion, and failures of repair as well as for later declarations repudiating them. Neighbor-love and acknowledgment of another ethos require hearing Jewish self-description and testing Christian representation by its historical fruits.

---

# With Other Religions

“Other religions” is an index, not an ethos. A valid study must identify the actual tradition, community, institution, representative, and transaction. It must also distinguish a New Testament relation to an ancient cult or philosophy from a present Christian relation to a living tradition.

Relevant interfaces include shared civic institutions, hospitality, trade, worship participation, mission, conversion, family, education, charity, environmental action, conflict mediation, and response to persecution. Paul's idol-food analysis already shows why these cannot be reduced to one rule: purchasing food, attending a ritual meal, receiving hospitality, and influencing another conscience have different actors and effects.

The comparison will use reciprocal evidence. Christian declarations and practice are tested by the same standard applied to the other ethos; each party's recognized sources and representatives are allowed to define its claims; and disagreements remain visible. Compatibility can be partial and action-specific without requiring doctrinal merger or hostility.

---

# Ethical Audit

The audit tests the reconstructions rather than assuming their coherence. It records which Theory of Ethos attributes are explicit, implied, absent, or contradicted; compares declarations within and across corpora; identifies missing governance or repair protocols; and separates a rule's textual existence from its historical enactment.

An audit result is not a verdict on a person or religion in the abstract. It is a claim about a specified version of an ethos under stated evidence. Each adverse finding must name the conflicting declarations or the declaration–action discrepancy, preserve counter-evidence, and remain open to challenge under the project's status system.

# Internal Contradictions

An internal contradiction exists when the same ethos assigns incompatible rules to the same actor and action under the same relevant conditions. Different narrative moments, addressees, institutions, or transactions may explain a tension, but those distinctions must be demonstrated rather than assumed.

Current cases for audit include:

| Tension | Proposed distinction | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “not against us” / “not with me” | unaffiliated compatible act / refusal in Jesus' contested act | tentative resolution |
| visible good works / secret practice | public outcome / status-seeking performance | strongly implied resolution |
| do not judge / community judgment | reciprocal self-implicating judgment / accountable correction | strongly implied resolution |
| honor family / leave or “hate” family | care obligation / ultimate allegiance | contested |
| Israel-restricted / widened mission | different audience and narrative moment | development; ethical rationale incomplete |
| receive prophet / do not test spirit / test claimant | utterance / sustained conduct | Didachean tension; tentative resolution |

Each case will store the exact witnesses, actor model, proposed distinction, counterexample, and consequence if unresolved. Canonical inclusion is not itself a resolution.

---

# Cross-Corpus Compatibility

Cross-corpus compatibility asks whether two witnessed ethoses can be enacted together, not whether an editor can make their vocabulary sound alike. The comparison proceeds in one direction at a time:

```text
Jesus baseline
→ post-death Christ enactment
→ Didachean codification
→ Pauline upgrade
→ later upgrade
→ present enacted ethos
```

For each transition the audit records preservation, specification, extension, reordering, replacement, and contradiction. Baptism after prior formation, for example, specifies joinability; Paul's body model redescribes membership; a later coercive initiation could contradict factual voluntary entry even while retaining baptismal words.

Gospel differences are audited before a composite Jesus claim is made. Acts and non-Pauline letters remain distinguishable inside the ethos of Christ. Disputed Pauline authorship is recorded rather than letting every letter attributed to Paul silently define the upgrade. Compatibility may also be partial: one community can share care of the poor while disagreeing about table or office.

---

# Missing Declarations

A missing declaration is a rule required to operate or test an ethos but not yet supported by the sources assigned to that layer. Absence must not be filled from later practice without labeling the upgrade.

Current candidates include:

- a complete public register of Jesus' governing declarations;
- a general amendment and appeal protocol;
- liability and material reparation for harm done by a representative;
- independent review of leaders and judges;
- safeguards for false accusations and vulnerable parties;
- durable selection and removal procedures for representatives;
- consent and exit rules for children, dependents, and households;
- a method for resolving conflicting witnesses and claimed signs;
- jurisdiction where internal discipline intersects civil harm;
- a proof protocol for translocal money and institutional authority.

Some later layers may supply one of these. The audit will then state whether the addition is compatible, whether it solves the earlier problem, and what new risks it creates. A source's silence is not automatically a moral failure, but claiming an ethically complete ethos while leaving the operational rule absent is challengeable.

---

# Historical Enactment

Historical enactment tests whether a declared Christian rule produced the claimed interaction when institutions possessed the opportunity and resources to act. It is distinct from reconstructing the rule's textual meaning. A failure of later Christians does not alter what Jesus said; it does alter claims that the later institution represented or successfully continued Jesus' ethos.

Each case study needs a bounded actor, date, location, declared rule, decision-maker, affected parties, available alternatives, action, outcome, evidence, and subsequent repair. Both beneficial and harmful enactments belong in the record. Selection only of exemplary saints or notorious abuses would distort the ethos actually sustained by ordinary institutions.

The main tests are saying versus doing, responsibility for representatives, fruits, use of resources, treatment of outsiders, challengeability, and reparation. Counterfactual judgment should be modest: the audit identifies feasible alternatives supported by contemporary evidence rather than assuming modern capacity in every past setting.

---

# Open Challenges

This register will contain unresolved objections to a named reconstruction claim. A challenge must specify the target declaration, source layer, evidence, type of defect, affected interaction, and proposed correction. The main defect types are internal contradiction, cross-layer incompatibility, unsupported inference, omitted counter-evidence, scope error, false representation, and declaration–action discrepancy.

Initial challenges include:

1. Does eschatological threat prevent Jesus' membership from qualifying as non-binding even where physical departure is allowed?
2. Can Jesus' ethos count as publicly stated when its reconstruction depends on later and differing Gospel narratives?
3. Does servant governance adequately limit binding/loosing authority without appeal or removal procedures?
4. Is the positive Golden Rule safe without a recipient-sensitive or shared-ethos qualification, and can that qualification be textually supported?
5. Does the Didache's prohibition on testing a spirit conflict with its behavioral test of prophets?
6. Does Paul preserve Jesus' outsider and enemy rules when regulating exclusion, civic authority, and idol relations?
7. Which later Christian institutions can prove legitimate representation of the earlier ethos rather than only historical succession?

An answered challenge updates the relevant page and remains in history; it is not silently deleted.

---

# Source Library

The source library provides alternate routes into the argument by saying, parable, action, beatitude, or New Testament book. These are evidence indexes, not independent ethoses. A source page preserves the distinct textual witnesses and links to the ethical declarations inferred from them; the governing claim remains in the ethos layer to which its speaker and setting belong.

This separation prevents a literary form from becoming the moral structure of the site. A parable may disclose a membership boundary, an action may prove a rule, and one saying may govern several kinds of interaction. None is classified primarily by a virtue.

# The Mustard Seed

The parable appears in [Matthew 13:31–32](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2013%3A31-32&version=ISV), [Mark 4:30–32](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%204%3A30-32&version=ISV), and [Luke 13:18–19](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2013%3A18-19&version=ISV). Each compares God's kingdom with a very small seed that becomes a large plant in which birds can nest; wording about garden, field, shrub, or tree differs among witnesses.

**Proposed ethos inference — `tentative`**

> `small_beginning_expands_shared_space`: The community governed by God's reign may begin in an unimpressive form and develop into a social space capable of receiving others.

The inference is limited. The stated comparison supports contrast between beginning and developed result; the birds make shelter or inclusion a possible social implication. It does not establish that an ethos has colors, is biologically invasive, grants legal immunity, or “nurtures higher forms of life.” Those properties come from modern descriptions of plants or imaginative extensions rather than the narrated ethical relation.

The seed's being called smallest is ordinary comparative speech in the story, not a universal botanical claim on which the ethos reconstruction depends. Allusions to scriptural tree-and-birds imagery may add kingdom and nations context, but must be argued separately for each Gospel.

This remains in the Source Library because it describes the kingdom's surprising development more directly than it states an interaction rule. It can enter the reconstructed ethos only when linked to clearer evidence about membership, hospitality, mission, or institutional growth.

# Beatitudes

The beatitudes in [Matthew 5:1–12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%205%3A1-12&version=ISV) and [Luke 6:20–26](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%206%3A20-26&version=ISV) are source units, not the architecture of Jesus' ethos. Matthew names conditions or qualities and promised outcomes; Luke pairs blessings with woes and speaks more directly of poverty, hunger, grief, and present social treatment. The two witnesses must not be merged into one virtue list.

Their primary ethical contributions are relational and constitutional questions:

- Which conditions does Jesus' proclamation recognize when ordinary status systems do not?
- Do promises to the poor, hungry, grieving, excluded, merciful, peacemaking, or persecuted create present duties for members and institutions?
- Who supplies the reversal, when, and through what resources or governance?
- Does persecution prove faithfulness, or must the cause and conduct also be tested?
- How do mercy and peacemaking relate to judgment, repair, and protection from harm?

**Current status — `under extraction`:** the beatitudes disclose the people and outcomes Jesus' ethos esteems, but many are not yet operating protocols. Virtues may annotate the ordering only after the corresponding interaction and responsible actor are identified.

# Actors and Institutions

Ethical analysis begins by identifying who acts personally, who represents an ethos, and who bears the result. Recurring actors include:

- **Jesus:** teacher and leader during his life; represented Christ after death must be kept analytically distinct.
- **Disciple/member:** an adherent in formation or a participant in a continuing community; not every hearer or beneficiary is a member.
- **The Twelve / apostle / emissary:** roles with different witnesses and scopes; the title alone does not prove a later claim of representation.
- **Prophet and teacher:** itinerant or local contributors tested by message, conduct, effect, and resources.
- **Host / household / family:** counterparties that supply resources, receive representatives, and include dependents with separate interests.
- **Assembly/church:** a corporate bearer capable of initiating, supporting, judging, appointing, sending, and being represented.
- **Bishop, elder, deacon, overseer:** office terms whose relation and stability change across layers.
- **Neighbor, stranger, enemy, outsider:** relation-specific positions, not permanent essences of a person.
- **Jew, Samaritan, Gentile:** historically situated group designations that must not be used as monolithic ethos labels.
- **Temple, synagogue, council, household, city, and empire:** institutions with distinct jurisdiction, representation, and resources.

Every page should state whether an actor is narrator, speaker, addressee, claimant, representative, judge, witness, beneficiary, harmed party, or institution. One actor may occupy several roles, but the transitions must remain visible.

# Glossary

**Ethos** — A governing Logos of a person or group whose principal content concerns what ought to be, including declarations, priorities, membership, representation, and sufficient continuity to guide action.

**Ethics / ethical** — The interaction rules between one or more ethoses. In this project, “ethical content” means content that defines or changes those rules or the ethos needed to enact them.

**Ethos of Jesus** — The ethos attributed to Jesus in his teaching and action during his life, reconstructed from distinguishable Gospel witnesses.

**Ethos of Christ** — The shared ethos enacted after Jesus' death by communities that belong to and act in the name of the risen Christ.

**Codification** — The organization of inherited declarations and practices into a portable rule for formation, initiation, recurring life, representation, resources, and governance. The Didache is treated as such a codification.

**Upgrade** — A later addition, specification, reordering, or replacement of an operating ethos rule. The word is descriptive until an ethical audit evaluates the change.

**Declaration** — A compact statement of a constitutive ethos rule, given a stable identifier such as `proof_by_fruits`.

**Protocol** — An ordered interaction specifying actors, steps, evidence, decision, and consequence.

**Representative** — A person or institution authorized to act or speak for another ethos bearer within a defined mandate.

**Enactment** — Observable action through which a declaration becomes operative and testable.

**Compatibility** — The degree to which two ethoses can perform a specified action together without violating their governing declarations. It may be partial and transaction-specific.

**Status** — The evidential classification attached to a reconstruction: `explicit`, `strongly implied`, `tentative`, `contested`, or `contradicted elsewhere`.

**Virtue** — A secondary evaluative quality or ordered end used where it helps explain an interaction; it is not the site's primary unit of organization.
