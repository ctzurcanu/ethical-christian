You are right. I made virtues and literary forms the organizing spine. That reverses the project.

The primary question is not:

> Which virtue does this saying teach?

It is:

> What does this saying reveal about the constitution, behavior, membership, governance, transmission, or inter-ethos relations of Jesus’ ethos?

Virtues matter mainly when a saying establishes an internal hierarchy or when two ethical declarations conflict.

## The project’s central object

The site should reconstruct and test:

> **The ethos of Jesus as witnessed by the New Testament.**

It should not initially assume that the entire New Testament expresses one coherent ethos. Distinguish:

- Jesus’ ethos as reconstructed from sayings and actions
- each evangelist’s presentation of that ethos
- the ethos of the early Jesus movement in Acts
- Pauline ethos
- the ethoses of other New Testament corpora
- the proposed canonical New Testament ethos

Only after extracting them separately should the site test whether they are compatible and whether a coherent canonical ethos can be composed from them.

This follows the theory’s own requirements: an ethos is public, internally coherent, ordered, identifiable, and challengeable—not merely a collection of admirable values.

## How every page should work

An article should extract an ethos declaration or protocol from one or more passages.

### 1. Proposed declaration

Begin with the result:

> `proof_by_fruits`: An ethos and its representatives are judged by their observable outcomes, not solely by their declarations.

This makes the page useful as part of the reconstructed ethos.

### 2. Textual witnesses

List the sayings and parallels separately. Preserve differences between evangelists rather than merging them into a synthetic quotation.

### 3. Ethical actors

Identify:

- whose ethos is speaking
- who is addressed
- whether the addressee acts personally or represents a group
- which other persons, ethoses, or institutions are involved

### 4. Interaction rule

State the ethical transaction:

```text
Jesus’ ethos → representative → external audience
external audience → response to representative → response to Jesus’ ethos
```

This is the core ethical analysis.

### 5. Ethos classification

Map it primarily to:

- meta-ethos principles
- phylogeny attributes
- hierarchy of intimacy
- compatibility rules
- one of the five ethical roles
- person–person, person–institution, or institution–institution interaction

### 6. Scope and conditions

Ask:

- Is this universal or only for disciples?
- Does it govern individuals or institutional representatives?
- Is compliance voluntary?
- What happens upon refusal?
- Is there an exit mechanism?
- What evidence establishes compliance?
- Who may judge it?

### 7. Compatibility and contradiction

Compare the declaration with:

- other sayings of Jesus
- other Gospel witnesses
- Pauline or later New Testament declarations
- the rest of the reconstructed Jesus ethos

### 8. Secondary virtue note

Only here add a short section such as:

> Secondary virtues implicated: Justice, Governance, Temperance.

This should occupy perhaps 10–20% of the article, not determine its organization.

### 9. Status

Each extracted declaration should be marked:

- `explicit`
- `strongly implied`
- `tentative`
- `contested`
- `contradicted elsewhere`

## Next sayings to cover

These are the most foundational omissions when the theory of ethos—not virtues—is primary.

### 1. “You will know them by their fruits”

**Matthew 7:15–23; Luke 6:43–46**

Proposed declaration:

> `proof_by_fruits`: The validity of a professed ethos is tested through its enacted outputs.

This should be next. It connects public declaration, proof, testability, representation, judgment, and the discrepancy between saying and doing.

“Not everyone who says to me, ‘Lord, Lord’” and “Why do you call me Lord and not do what I tell you?” make the point even clearer: naming an ethos is not the same as living it.

### 2. “No one can serve two masters”

**Matthew 6:24; Luke 16:13**, together with **Mark 3:24–27**

Proposed declaration:

> `one_ethos`: A person cannot sustainably enact two incompatible ruling ethoses.

Cover:

- one-ethos identity
- divided institutional ethos
- contradiction
- hierarchy of allegiance
- whether family, wealth, state, and God are subordinate ethoses or competitors

Virtues are secondary here; the central issue is unity of governance.

### 3. Community correction and witnesses

**Matthew 18:15–20**

This passage almost directly specifies an ethical protocol:

```text
private challenge
→ witnessed challenge
→ institutional hearing
→ compatibility judgment
→ change of relationship
```

It should be analyzed against:

- `challengeable`
- `witnesses`
- `judge_invite`
- `governance`
- `representation`
- institutional authority
- escalation proportionality

It is one of the most important passages for this entire project.

### 4. “Whoever receives you receives me”

**Matthew 10:40–42; Luke 10:16; John 13:20**

Proposed declaration:

> `representation`: Treatment of an authorized representative is treatment of the represented ethos.

This covers:

- proof of authority
- ambassadorial representation
- collective responsibility
- limits of delegated authority
- the meaning of acting “in my name”

It also provides a stronger foundation for the existing Golden Rule page’s claim that disciples act as representatives of an ethos.

### 5. Sending the Twelve and the Seventy

**Matthew 10; Mark 6:7–13; Luke 9:1–6; 10:1–16**

This is the clearest operational picture of missionary and ambassadorial ethos.

Cover:

- missionary responsibility
- working in pairs as mutual witnesses
- awareness of resource limitations
- dependence on reciprocal hospitality
- temporary entry into another household’s ethos
- non-coercive response to rejection
- withdrawal and “shaking off the dust”
- responsibility of the sender for representatives

### 6. Servant governance

**Mark 10:42–45; Matthew 20:25–28; Luke 22:24–27**

Proposed declaration:

> `servant_governance`: Authority within Jesus’ ethos is justified by service rather than domination.

Compare the governance of Jesus’ group with the governance of surrounding institutions. Ask whether this eliminates hierarchy or redefines the proof and obligations of authority.

John 13:12–17 supplies enacted proof: the leader models the rule before binding followers to it.

### 7. “Whoever is not against us is for us” / “Whoever is not with me is against me”

**Mark 9:38–41; Luke 9:49–50; Matthew 12:30; Luke 11:23**

These should share one page.

They directly test:

- ethos compatibility
- partial alignment
- authorization and name-use
- recognition of unaffiliated actors
- boundary between neutral, compatible, and hostile ethoses
- possible internal contradiction

Do not harmonize them before identifying differences in actor, action, and context.

### 8. “The Sabbath was made for humankind”

**Mark 2:23–3:6 and parallels**

Proposed declaration:

> `law_is_challengeable`: A law remains subordinate to the purpose and life it was intended to sustain.

This is about:

- flexible ethos
- challengeable law
- testability through consequences
- authority to reinterpret inherited ethos
- relation between Jesus’ ethos and an institutional interpretation of Judaism

The healing question—whether it is lawful to do good or harm, save life or kill—turns legal interpretation into an ethos compatibility test.

### 9. “Render to Caesar”

**Mark 12:13–17 and parallels**

Treat this as an interaction between institutional ethoses:

- individual
- Jesus movement
- Temple/Jewish authority
- Roman state
- divine authority

Ask what belongs to each, how authority is proven, and what happens when the claims overlap. Avoid reducing it to obedience or separation of church and state.

### 10. Joining, cost, and exit

Use together:

- **Mark 8:34–38** — take up the cross
- **Luke 9:57–62** — conditions of following
- **Luke 14:25–33** — count the cost
- **John 6:60–67** — disciples leave, and the Twelve are asked whether they also wish to leave

Proposed questions:

- Is Jesus’ ethos joinable?
- Are joining conditions public?
- Is consent sufficiently informed?
- Is membership non-binding?
- May a member leave freely?
- Does leaving carry threats or penalties?
- Does the ethos compose with the nuclear family?

John 6 is especially important evidence for a non-coercive exit: Jesus permits departure and asks the remaining disciples to choose.

### 11. Love of enemies

**Matthew 5:38–48; Luke 6:27–36**

This belongs primarily under interaction with hostile ethoses, not under “virtues.”

Analyze:

- behavior toward an incompatible ethos
- positive action without prior reciprocity
- impartiality
- generosity
- interruption of retaliation cycles
- whether the rule applies to persons, representatives, or institutions
- whether non-retaliation suspends justice or only personal vengeance

### 12. The Greatest Commandments

**Mark 12:28–34; Matthew 22:34–40; Luke 10:25–28**

This establishes the top of the reconstructed ethos:

> loyalty to God and treatment of the neighbor govern the interpretation of subordinate declarations.

The virtue hierarchy is relevant, but secondary. The primary result is constitutional: Jesus states which declarations the rest of the ethos depends upon.

### 13. The Good Samaritan

**Luke 10:29–37**

Treat it as ethical empathy between socially opposed ethoses:

```text
recognition
→ discrimination by action rather than identity
→ costly assistance
→ continuing institutional care
→ obligation to imitate
```

The saying redefines “neighbor” by enacted cooperation rather than inherited group identity.

### 14. Public proof versus secret practice

Read together:

- **Matthew 5:14–16** — visible good works
- **Matthew 6:1–18** — hidden almsgiving, prayer, and fasting

This is an important apparent tension:

- must an ethos be public?
- must its actions be provable?
- when does public proof become reputation-seeking performance?
- can proof be available without publishing the receiver’s identity?
- what distinguishes a public ethos from public self-promotion?

This may materially refine the theory’s relationship between proof, privacy, charity, and recognition.

### 15. The Great Commission

**Matthew 28:18–20**

Analyze it as institutional continuity:

- missionary ethos
- joining ritual
- formation of representatives
- teaching the whole ethos rather than isolated beliefs
- responsibility for descendants
- continuity of authority
- possible tension between invitation and coercive historical enactment

## The first coherent publication sequence

I would write the next pages in this order:

1. Known by Their Fruits  
2. No One Can Serve Two Masters  
3. Challenge, Witnesses, and the Community  
4. Whoever Receives You Receives Me  
5. Sending the Twelve and the Seventy  
6. Servant Governance  
7. For Us, Against Us, or Compatible  
8. Sabbath and Challengeable Law  
9. Joining, Leaving, and Counting the Cost  
10. Love of Enemies  

Those ten pages would establish the main architecture of Jesus’ ethos: identity, proof, challenge, representation, mission, governance, compatibility, flexibility, membership, and relations with opponents. The virtue mappings can then annotate that reconstruction without displacing it.
